# Bytes, Not FLOPs, Now Steer Cloud AI

As of October 2026, the most useful thing an AI engineer can know about the cloud is that most hard decisions come down to storing and moving bytes, not to doing arithmetic. That holds for which accelerator to rent, how to shard a training run, how many users one serving replica can hold, and what an agent costs. LLM decode is limited by memory bandwidth. Mixed-precision training carries about **16 bytes of state per parameter**. The KV cache often outweighs the model weights. The size of the NVLink domain (**8 GPUs in an HGX node, 72 in an NVL72 rack**) now matters as much as which GPU you pick. Around these constraints the stack has largely standardized. On hardware, that means Hopper and Blackwell GPUs, with 288 GB Blackwell Ultra widely rentable and Vera Rubin in limited availability since 30 September 2026. On orchestration, it means Kubernetes with Dynamic Resource Allocation (DRA) generally available, plus Kueue, JobSet or Slurm for gang-scheduled jobs. Serving runs on vLLM, SGLang or TensorRT-LLM behind inference-aware gateways, tools connect over MCP (the Model Context Protocol, which became stateless in July 2026), and traces go through OpenTelemetry. The economics are lopsided in a way that should set priorities. GPU rental prices rose through 2026: AWS raised Capacity Block rates three times, about 59% compounded. Yet vendor telemetry finds average GPU utilization near **5%** in unoptimized Kubernetes clusters, and Anthropic measures agent loops costing **1.7–7.3× more without prompt caching**. Raising utilization and cache hit rates therefore beats any rate discount. Regulation moved as well. The EU's Digital Omnibus deferred high-risk obligations to December 2027, but enforcement for general-purpose AI (GPAI) models and the Article 50 transparency duties took effect in August 2026. Treat many price and performance figures below as approximate: much of the underlying research relied on aggregators and vendor claims, and conflicts are flagged where they matter.

## The infrastructure layer: memory, interconnect and data gravity decide placement

The bottom of the stack saw 2026's biggest shifts: new memory tiers, rack-scale NVLink, rising prices, and Kubernetes finally treating accelerators as first-class devices. The three areas below cover what to rent, how to schedule it, and where the data should live.

### Compute: rent HBM capacity and NVLink domains, then buy capacity like a commodity

The rental market in October 2026 still centers on NVIDIA Hopper (H100, H200) and Blackwell (B200, GB200). **Blackwell Ultra (B300/GB300, 288 GB HBM3e per GPU) is now widely rentable.** **Vera Rubin (288 GB HBM4 at about 22 TB/s, NVLink 6 at 3.6 TB/s per GPU) has been in limited availability at CoreWeave since 30 September 2026.** Its first customer there, Cognition, reports 4.8× token throughput over a GB200 NVL72 baseline. That is a customer claim, not an independent benchmark ([CoreWeave](https://www.coreweave.com/news/coreweave-delivers-nvidia-vera-rubin-nvl72-performance-at-production-scale-starting-with-cognition); [Barrack.ai](https://blog.barrack.ai/nvidia-rubin-specs-architecture-2026/)). NVIDIA said Rubin began shipping to AWS, Google Cloud, Azure and OCI in July. No hyperscaler Rubin instance was confirmed generally available by mid-October, though, and one vendor expects most teams to wait until 2027 ([Spheron](https://www.spheron.network/blog/vera-rubin-nvl72-cloud-availability-cost-per-token/)). AMD's current cloud part is the **MI355X (288 GB HBM3E, ~8 TB/s, ~10 PF dense FP4)**, generally available on OCI and Vultr. The older MI300X (192 GB) remains common on Azure ([SemiAnalysis InferenceX](https://inferencex.semianalysis.com/chips/mi355x); [Microsoft Docs: ND MI300X v5](https://github.com/MicrosoftDocs/azure-compute-docs/blob/main/articles/virtual-machines/sizes/gpu-accelerated/ndmi300xv5-series.md)).

The custom-silicon rows come from Google, AWS and Microsoft documents cited below.

Two patterns in that table drive selection. First, **per-GPU memory now comes in three tiers (80 GB, 141–192 GB and 288 GB), and HBM bandwidth roughly doubles each generation (3.4 → 4.8 → 8 → ~22 TB/s)**. Decode re-reads weights and KV cache on every step. So for chat and long-context serving, bandwidth and capacity per dollar matter more than peak FLOPs, which matter mostly for prefill and training. Second, **the NVLink domain has become a selection criterion in its own right**. NVLink 4 gives 450 GB/s per direction inside an 8-GPU Hopper node. NVLink 5 doubles that to 900 GB/s and stretches the domain to **72 GPUs in a GB200 NVL72 rack**. Scale-out across racks stays near 400 Gb/s, about 50 GB/s, per GPU ([Scaling Book](https://github.com/jax-ml/scaling-book/blob/main/gpus.md)). Azure's GB300 VM shows the shape concretely: four 288 GB GPUs per VM, NVLink at 1.8 TB/s across up to 72 GPUs, and 4 × 800 Gb/s InfiniBand "to up to 100,000 GPUs" ([Microsoft Docs](https://github.com/MicrosoftDocs/azure-compute-docs/blob/main/articles/virtual-machines/sizes/gpu-accelerated/nd-gb300-v6-series.md)). For mixture-of-experts (MoE) serving, or any tensor or expert parallelism wider than eight GPUs, an NVL72-class domain keeps all-to-all traffic off the slower network.

Custom silicon gives up CUDA's ecosystem for better price-performance, and in 2026 it finally became easy to price. **Google's TPU7x Ironwood is generally available at a public list price of $12.00 per chip-hour on demand in us-central1.** That falls to $6.00 under Dynamic Workload Scheduler (DWS) Flex-start and $5.40 on a three-year commitment. Trillium (v6e) lists at $2.70 ([Cloud TPU pricing](https://cloud.google.com/tpu/pricing); [Scaling Book: TPUs](https://github.com/jax-ml/scaling-book/blob/main/tpus.md)). Dividing price by FP8 peak gives the comparison. On-demand Ironwood costs about $2.6 per FP8 petaflop-hour. A B200 bought through DWS Flex-start costs about $1.8. Ironwood under Flex-start or a three-year commitment costs about $1.2–1.3. TPUs therefore win mainly under commitment or queued capacity, and at scales where a 9,216-chip interconnect domain avoids an InfiniBand tier. **AWS Trainium3 (144 GiB at 4.9 TB/s, 2.5 PF MXFP8) ships in UltraServers of up to 144 chips on an all-to-all switched fabric.** The design targets MoE serving and mirrors NVL72 ([Neuron docs: Trainium3](https://github.com/aws-neuron/aws-neuron-sdk/blob/master/about-neuron/arch/neuron-hardware/trainium3.rst); [Neuron docs: Trn3 architecture](https://github.com/aws-neuron/aws-neuron-sdk/blob/master/about-neuron/arch/neuron-hardware/trn3-arch.rst)). Anthropic is a named customer, and Bedrock serves production traffic on it ([Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251201046353/en/Trainium3-UltraServers-Now-Available-Enabling-Customers-to-Train-and-Deploy-AI-Models-Faster-at-Lower-Cost)). **Azure's Maia 200 is an inference chip** that serves GPT-5.2, Microsoft Foundry and Microsoft 365 Copilot. You consume it through Azure services rather than renting it as a VM ([Microsoft](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/01/26/maia-200-the-ai-accelerator-built-for-inference/)). Porting cost lives in software. JAX/XLA is first-class on TPU and the Neuron SDK on Trainium; custom CUDA kernels are what break.

Sizing starts with arithmetic every AI engineer should do reflexively. **Inference weights ≈ parameters × bytes per parameter.** That is 2 bytes for BF16, 1 for FP8 or INT8, and 0.5 for FP4 or INT4. The KV cache adds **2 × layers × KV heads × head dimension × bytes per element, for every token in context**. A single 8,192-token LLaMA-13B sequence in BF16 already needs 6.7 GB, and "just 4 of these exceed the memory usage of our parameters" ([Scaling Book: inference](https://github.com/jax-ml/scaling-book/blob/main/inference.md)). Training with Adam needs **at least 10 bytes per parameter** with BF16 weights and FP32 moments. Classic mixed precision with FP32 master weights needs **16 bytes**. Both figures exclude activations, which often dominate ([Scaling Book: training](https://github.com/jax-ml/scaling-book/blob/main/training.md); [ZeRO, Rajbhandari et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02054)). The consequences are concrete. A 70B model in BF16 (140 GB) does not fit one 80 GB H100 and is borderline on a 141 GB H200. It does fit a single MI300X, B200, B300 or MI355X with KV headroom, and in FP8 it fits comfortably on an H200. A 7B full fine-tune needs about 112 GB of state, which overflows one H100 unless you shard, offload or use LoRA. Leave 20–40% headroom beyond the weights for KV cache, activations and CUDA graphs.

Purchasing has split into four modes with very different risk profiles. The first is **short-term guaranteed capacity**: AWS EC2 Capacity Blocks and GCP DWS Calendar mode. Calendar mode runs 1–90 days, comes only in 8-GPU shapes, and is paid in full even if unused ([GCP DWS pricing](https://cloud.google.com/products/dws/pricing)). It suits training runs with a hard deadline. The second is **queued flexible capacity** through DWS Flex-start. It bills like on-demand once provisioned, and for TPUs it costs half the on-demand rate. The third is **long commitments**, up to 55% off on GCP three-year resource committed-use discounts (CUDs). GPU commitments need attached reservations to guarantee zonal capacity, and spend-based flexible CUDs do not cover GPUs at all ([GCP GPU pricing](https://cloud.google.com/compute/gpus-pricing)). The fourth is **spot**, up to 91% off on GCP. It comes with a **2-minute notice on AWS and only 30 seconds on Azure** ([AWS EC2 guide](https://github.com/awsdocs/amazon-ec2-user-guide/blob/master/doc_source/spot-instance-termination-notices.md); [Azure Spot VMs](https://github.com/MicrosoftDocs/azure-compute-docs/blob/main/articles/virtual-machines/spot-vms.md)). Neither window is long enough to write a large checkpoint, so periodic asynchronous checkpointing is mandatory.

The defining trend of 2026 is scarcity. AWS raised Capacity Block prices about **15% on 4 January, 20% on 1 July and 15% on 7 October 2026**. Third-party trackers put P6-B300 at about $16.15 and P5 (H100) at about $5.97 per GPU-hour, a roughly 59% compounded rise for the year ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/05/aws_price_increase/); [ecorpit](https://ecorpit.com/aws-ec2-capacity-blocks-gpu-price-rise-finops-2026/); [FastGPU](https://dev.to/fastgpu/15-gpu-list-prices-rose-in-september-and-none-fell-today-aws-raises-capacity-blocks-15-44l)). One tracker counted 15 GPU list-price increases and no decreases in September alone.

The spreads are large. Neocloud on-demand H100s cost about $2.5–4 per GPU-hour against $5–7 at hyperscalers, and B200s about $6.7–8.6 against $8–14. The hyperscaler premium buys integration (IAM, VPC, compliance and managed services such as Bedrock or Foundry), not faster silicon. Neoclouds get new silicon first; CoreWeave was first with Rubin. They often sell in 8-GPU node bundles, such as CoreWeave's HGX H100 node at $49.24/hour ([CloudZero on CoreWeave](https://www.cloudzero.com/blog/coreweave-pricing/)). Egress fees, minimum commitments and term contracts can change the real cost more than the headline rate ([Spheron comparison](https://www.spheron.network/blog/gpu-cloud-pricing-comparison-2026/)). A sensible ladder follows. Use a Capacity Block or Calendar reservation for a deadline-bound training run, but note that after the 2026 hikes, AWS B200 and B300 blocks ($14–16) can cost more than neocloud on-demand ($6–9). Use Flex-start or spot for tolerant batch work and fine-tunes, and commitments for a steady inference fleet. Keep the stack portable (containers plus Kubernetes or Slurm) so you can source from two or three providers.

Autoscaling GPU workloads is mostly a fight against cold start. Image pull, weight download, loading into HBM, and compilation or CUDA-graph capture together take tens of seconds to minutes. Kubernetes' Cluster Autoscaler counts only GPU requests on GPU nodes, with a **default scale-down threshold of 0.5**. An 8-GPU node with three GPUs requested is therefore a scale-down candidate, which can make bin-packed inference fleets thrash ([Cluster Autoscaler FAQ](https://github.com/kubernetes/autoscaler/blob/master/cluster-autoscaler/FAQ.md)). Karpenter on AWS watches an SQS interruption queue and provisions a replacement in parallel while draining a spot node ([Karpenter disruption docs](https://github.com/aws/karpenter-provider-aws/blob/main/website/content/en/docs/concepts/disruption.md)). KEDA scales event-driven workloads to and from zero ([KEDA](https://github.com/kedacore/keda)). Serverless GPU platforms package the mitigations. Modal caches weights and vLLM compilation artifacts in Volumes, cutting startup "to about ten seconds when loaded from the cache," and offers experimental GPU memory snapshots ([Modal vLLM example](https://github.com/modal-labs/modal-examples/blob/main/06_gpu_and_ml/llm-serving/vllm_inference.py); [Modal GPU snapshots](https://github.com/modal-labs/modal-examples/blob/main/06_gpu_and_ml/gpu_snapshot.py)). RunPod bills worker start time but not image pull, defaults to a 5-second idle timeout, and offers FlashBoot plus minimum active workers ([RunPod pricing](https://github.com/runpod/docs/blob/main/serverless/pricing.mdx); [RunPod overview](https://github.com/runpod/docs/blob/main/serverless/overview.mdx)). Google Cloud Run now bills GPUs per second, **about $0.67/hour for an L4 and about $1.31/hour for an RTX PRO 6000**. Google's worked example puts an L4 service peaking at two instances at about $822/month ([Cloud Run pricing](https://cloud.google.com/run/pricing)). The break-even logic is simple. One always-warm H100 replica costs roughly $1.8k–5k a month, so scale-to-zero pays only when traffic is spiky and a queue can hide the cold start.

### Containers and orchestration: Kubernetes absorbed the GPU, but gangs still need add-ons

The mental model to hold has three parts. The host owns the kernel driver, the image carries the CUDA user-space libraries, and the NVIDIA Container Toolkit injects device nodes and host driver libraries when the container starts. Since 2025 the toolkit does this through the **Container Device Interface (CDI), the default since GPU Operator 25.10**. One gotcha follows: workloads that rely on the `NVIDIA_VISIBLE_DEVICES` environment variable must now set `runtimeClassName: nvidia` ([GPU Operator release notes](https://github.com/NVIDIA/cloud-native-docs/blob/main/gpu-operator/release-notes.rst)). Toolkit v1.20.1 (19 September 2026) adds per-GPU CUDA memory limits. They require R615+ drivers and are exposed in GPU Operator 26.7.1 through `NVIDIA_GPU_MEMORY_REQUEST` and `NVIDIA_GPU_MEMORY_LIMIT` ([NVIDIA Container Toolkit releases](https://github.com/NVIDIA/nvidia-container-toolkit/releases)). Security matters here as well. **CVE-2025-23266 ("NVIDIAScape") is a CVSS 9.0 container escape fixed in toolkit 1.17.8 and GPU Operator 25.3.2.** Any cluster running older versions has a critical exposure ([NVIDIA security bulletin](https://nvidia.custhelp.com/app/answers/detail/a_id/5659/~/security-bulletin:-nvidia-container-toolkit---july-2025)). The biggest packaging change is that **Kubernetes 1.36 made OCI image volumes stable**. The kubelet can now mount model weights from a registry as a digest-pinned volume, separate from the application image, which requires containerd 2.1 or later ([Kubernetes v1.36 release](https://github.com/kubernetes/website/blob/main/content/en/blog/_posts/2026/kubernetes-v1-36-release/index.md); [arXiv 2607.16596](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.16596v1)). The practical rule: bake models under 1–2 GB into the image. Mount multi-gigabyte weights from image volumes, node-local NVMe caches, or Lustre and object-storage volumes. A 100 GB image layer per model version slows every rebuild and defeats registry deduplication.

GPU allocation is in transition. The classic device plugin, which exposes the `nvidia.com/gpu` extended resource, is still the most widely deployed path. It has one notorious gotcha: a pod that requests no GPUs on a GPU node sees all of them ([NVIDIA k8s-device-plugin](https://github.com/NVIDIA/k8s-device-plugin)). Sharing comes in three grades. Time-slicing has no memory isolation and a shared fault domain, so it belongs in dev environments and notebooks only. MPS partitions memory equally among clients, but it is still experimental and unsupported on MIG devices. MIG provides hardware isolation with up to seven instances per GPU and is the right choice for multi-tenant production inference. Meanwhile **DRA reached general availability in Kubernetes 1.34** ([Kubernetes blog](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2025/09/01/kubernetes-v1-34-dra-updates/)). Kubernetes 1.37 (26 August 2026) made two more DRA features stable. The extended-resource bridge lets tenants keep requesting `nvidia.com/gpu` while a DRA driver serves the request. Device taints with a cluster-wide `DeviceTaintRule` give a native way to fence off a bad GPU ([Kubernetes v1.37 DRA updates](https://github.com/kubernetes/website/blob/main/content/en/blog/_posts/2026/kubernetes-v1-37-dra-updates.md)). NVIDIA donated its DRA driver to the CNCF at KubeCon EU 2026 ([NVIDIA](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-at-kubecon-2026/)). The driver's own README, however, says only **ComputeDomains (multi-node NVLink for GB200-class racks) are officially supported; per-GPU allocation through DRA is "not yet officially supported"** ([k8s-dra-driver-gpu](https://github.com/NVIDIA/k8s-dra-driver-gpu)). In practice, use the device plugin for whole-GPU pods today and DRA where NVL72 racks require it.

Gang scheduling, which places all of a job's pods at once or none of them, is still bolted on. Kubernetes' native Workload and PodGroup API reached Beta in 1.37, but **the `GenericWorkload` feature gate is off by default**. JobSet integration is targeted for 1.38, and Kueue does not yet integrate with it. Some outlets wrongly reported it as on by default ([Kubernetes v1.37 workload-aware scheduling](https://github.com/kubernetes/website/blob/main/content/en/blog/_posts/2026/kubernetes-v1-37-advancing-workload-aware-scheduling.md)). The production path is add-ons. Kueue v0.20.1 (8 October 2026) handles quota, admission, fair sharing, topology-aware placement and multi-cluster dispatch. Its v0.20.0 release stopped serving the v1beta1 API, so migrate before upgrading ([Kueue releases](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/kueue/releases)). JobSet runs a group of Jobs as one unit with failure and success policies ([JobSet](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/jobset)). LeaderWorkerSet handles inference replicas that span multiple hosts, with all-or-nothing group restart. Its new DisaggregatedSet, co-designed with llm-d, splits prefill and decode ([LWS](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/lws)). Volcano (CNCF incubating) and NVIDIA's KAI Scheduler (CNCF sandbox, from Run:ai) supply gang scheduling, hierarchical queues and topology-aware placement ([Volcano](https://github.com/volcano-sh/volcano); [KAI Scheduler](https://github.com/NVIDIA/KAI-Scheduler)).

| Multi-node NVLink on GB200/GB300 racks | NVIDIA DRA driver ComputeDomains | The only officially supported DRA path |

The managed Kubernetes services now productize GPU node lifecycle. **EKS Auto Mode cut its GPU management fees from 1 July 2026, by 35% for G-series and 60% for P-series and Trainium instances.** It also includes accelerator-aware node repair ([AWS What's New](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/07/amazon-eks-auto-mode-gpu-price/)). Its node monitoring agent sets an `AcceleratedHardwareReady` condition, and repair begins 10 minutes after detection while respecting PodDisruptionBudgets ([AWS containers blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/containers/how-to-run-ai-model-inference-with-gpus-on-amazon-eks-auto-mode)). GKE Autopilot places pods on B200, H200, H100 and A100 nodes through ComputeClasses ([GKE docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/kubernetes-engine/docs/how-to/autopilot-gpus)). GKE Inference Gateway integrates llm-d scheduling and multi-cluster routing, though Google's benchmark claims for it are vendor-reported ([Google Cloud blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/ai-infrastructure/whats-new-in-ai-infrastructure-this-month)). The upstream Gateway API Inference Extension it implements declares itself GA, and its endpoint picker has moved into llm-d repositories ([Gateway API Inference Extension](https://github.com/kubernetes-sigs/gateway-api-inference-extension)). AKS offers the KAITO add-on, which requires Ampere-generation or newer GPUs ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/aks/ai-toolchain-operator)). SageMaker HyperPod runs on EKS or Slurm with deep health checks, automatic node recovery and Kueue-based task governance ([HyperPod CLI](https://github.com/aws/sagemaker-hyperpod-cli)).

Above Kubernetes, three ecosystems matter. Ray became a PyTorch Foundation project in October 2025 ([PyTorch Foundation](https://pytorch.org/blog/pytorch-foundation-welcomes-ray-to-deliver-a-unified-open-source-ai-compute-stack/)). **Ray 2.59.0 (2 October 2026) made Ray Data LLM and Ray Serve LLM generally available**, and turned token authentication on by default for local clusters ([Ray releases](https://github.com/ray-project/ray/releases)). That last default speaks to Ray's historical security posture: never expose Ray dashboards or job APIs to the internet. KubeRay 1.7 added incremental RayService upgrades and deprecated `ray.io/v1alpha1` ([KubeRay releases](https://github.com/ray-project/kuberay/releases)). Use Ray for heterogeneous Python pipelines such as batch embedding, RL rollouts, hyperparameter sweeps and multi-model serving. For fixed-shape SPMD pretraining (every GPU running the same step in lockstep), torchrun with JobSet or Slurm is simpler. Kubeflow reorganized. **Trainer v2 (TrainJob built on JobSet; v2.3.0 in August 2026) supersedes Training Operator v1**, which now gets only bug fixes ([Kubeflow Trainer releases](https://github.com/kubeflow/trainer/releases)). Kubeflow Pipelines is at 2.17.2; its 2.15 release replaced MinIO with SeaweedFS as the default object store and removed ML Metadata (MLMD) ([KFP releases](https://github.com/kubeflow/pipelines/releases)). KServe v0.21 (25 September 2026) is now LLM-centric, with `LLMInferenceService`, KEDA scaling and DRA claims ([KServe releases](https://github.com/kserve/kserve/releases)). Slurm remains the scheduler of choice for large training clusters. **NVIDIA acquired SchedMD, Slurm's developer, in December 2025** and pledged to keep it open source and vendor-neutral. NVIDIA says Slurm runs on more than half of the TOP500's top 10 and top 100 systems ([Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/4106930/nvidia-moves-deeper-into-ai-infrastruct-with-schedmd-acquisition.html)). The Slinky slurm-operator runs Slurm's controller and worker daemons as Kubernetes custom resources. It drains workers in Slurm before terminating their pods and can scale to zero ([Slinky slurm-operator](https://github.com/SlinkyProject/slurm-operator)). The pragmatic split is Slurm for big pretraining and researcher batch, Kubernetes for inference and pipelines, and hybrids that share one GPU pool. One vendor-neutrality flag: NVIDIA now shapes Slurm, KAI and Dynamo, and it wrote the DRA driver it donated.

Health handling has native building blocks but vendor glue. Kubernetes 1.36 promoted device health in pod status (`allocatedResourcesStatus`) to Beta ([Kubernetes v1.36](https://github.com/kubernetes/website/blob/main/content/en/blog/_posts/2026/kubernetes-v1-36-release/index.md)). Kubernetes 1.37 made DRA device taints stable and added node lifecycle conditions as alpha, though no core controller acts on them yet ([Kubernetes node lifecycle conditions](https://github.com/kubernetes/website/blob/main/content/en/blog/_posts/2026/kubernetes-v1-37-node-lifecycle-conditions.md)). The automation that actually cordons and replaces bad GPUs still comes from managed services, such as EKS repair and HyperPod deep health checks, or from DCGM-based tooling wired into Kueue or Slurm.

### Data and storage: object storage holds the truth; indexes, caches and Lustre are disposable projections

Regional object storage is the durable source of truth for nearly all AI data. S3 has offered strong read-after-write consistency since December 2020 ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2020/12/amazon-s3-now-delivers-strong-read-after-write-consistency-automatically-for-all-applications)). It scales by parallelism across key prefixes: **at least 3,500 writes and 5,500 reads per second per partitioned prefix**. Auto-partitioning after a load spike returns 503 "Slow Down" errors until it completes, so clients must retry with backoff ([AWS knowledge center](https://repost.aws/knowledge-center/s3-object-key-naming-pattern)). Every hyperscaler now also sells a zonal, low-latency tier for data sitting next to accelerators. S3 Express One Zone cut prices in April 2025 (storage −31%, PUTs −55%, GETs −85%) and handles up to 2 million GETs per second per directory bucket ([AWS News Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/es/blogs/aws/up-to-85-price-reductions-for-amazon-s3-express-one-zone/)). GCS Rapid Storage offers sub-millisecond latency, up to 15 TB/s and 20 million QPS, plus appendable objects suited to checkpoints and logs ([GCS Rapid docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/storage/docs/rapid/high-performance-storage)). Google's reference pattern is explicit: the regional bucket is durable and the zonal layer is cache or scratch ([GCS AI zones](https://docs.cloud.google.com/storage/docs/ai-zones)). Single-AZ tiers should therefore hold only data you can recreate.

For training data, shard into 100 MB–1 GB sequential objects. **Sequential 100 MB reads reach about 80% of object-store bandwidth, while random 1 KB reads reach about 1%** ([ZeroEntropy](https://zeroentropy.dev/concepts/streaming-datasets/)). MosaicML StreamingDataset (MDS format) streams from any major cloud with deterministic, resumable multi-node loading ([PyPI: mosaicml-streaming](https://pypi.org/project/mosaicml-streaming)). WebDataset tar shards are the simpler alternative. Text LLM pretraining is rarely I/O-bound: 15T tokens stored as 2-byte IDs is about 30 TB per epoch. Multimodal data, small-file random access and checkpoint bursts are what strain storage. Egress, not storage, usually decides the bill. Entry internet egress runs about $0.09/GB on AWS, $0.087 on Azure and $0.12 on GCP's Premium tier, against $0 on Cloudflare R2 ([eCorpIT](https://ecorpit.com/cloud-egress-fees-ai-inference-bill-comparison-2026/)). Hyperscalers waive egress only for customers leaving their platform ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/09/10/big_clouds_scramble_over_eu/)). Moving a 100 TB dataset out of AWS costs roughly $8–9k, a real cost for multi-cloud "follow the GPUs" strategies.

Managed Lustre is now the standard fast POSIX tier on all three hyperscalers. **FSx for Lustre reaches 1,200 Gbps per client using EFA (AWS's Elastic Fabric Adapter) with GPUDirect Storage.** Its Intelligent-Tiering file systems provision up to 2 TB/s independently of capacity ([FSx Lustre performance](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/fsx/latest/LustreGuide/performance.html); [FSx throughput capacity](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/fsx/latest/LustreGuide/managing-throughput-capacity.html)). Google Cloud Managed Lustre went GA on 30 June 2025 with tiers from 125 to 1,000 MB/s per TiB and up to 10 TB/s aggregate; doc versions disagree on the maximum ([GCP Managed Lustre tiers](https://docs.cloud.google.com/managed-lustre/docs/performance-tiers)). Azure Managed Lustre offers 40–500 MB/s per TiB ([Azure pricing](https://www.azure.cn/en-us/pricing/details/managed-lustre/index.html)). You need a parallel file system for four things: small-file or random access you cannot re-shard, very high aggregate bandwidth for video training, fast synchronous checkpoints, and HPC-style scratch. Otherwise object storage plus streaming is cheaper. The checkpoint math makes the stakes clear. A full training state of about 16 bytes per parameter is about 1.1 TB for a 70B model. That takes 10 seconds to write at 100 GB/s, but 100 seconds of idle GPUs at 10 GB/s. Per-TiB throughput pricing also often forces you to buy capacity to get bandwidth: a 50 TB working set that needs 50 GB/s requires the 1,000 MB/s/TiB tier. The usual pattern keeps the master copy in object storage and runs Lustre as a hydrated cache for the job, torn down afterward.

Lakehouses are where curated training data, evaluation sets, features and traces live. **Apache Iceberg v3 adds deletion vectors, row lineage and a VARIANT type**, and upgrading a table to v3 is one-way ([Google Open Source Blog](https://opensource.googleblog.com/2025/08/whats-new-in-iceberg-v3.html?m=0)). Databricks Unity Catalog supports v3 on Databricks Runtime 18 LTS and later, with Delta interoperability ([Databricks](https://www.databricks.com/en/blog/iceberg-v3-moving-ecosystem-towards-unification)). Snowflake reportedly made v3 GA on 7 May 2026 ([Atlan](https://atlan.com/know/snowflake/apache-iceberg-v3/)). With formats converging, lock-in has moved from the file format to the catalog and governance layer. Each platform now bundles AI primitives next to the data. BigQuery's `VECTOR_SEARCH` supports IVF and ScaNN-based TreeAH indexes alongside in-SQL `AI.EMBED` ([BigQuery vector index docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/bigquery/docs/vector-index)). Databricks launched Lakebase, a Neon-based managed Postgres ([Databricks](https://www.databricks.com/company/newsroom/press-releases/databricks-launches-lakebase-new-class-operational-database-ai-apps)), and agreed in August 2025 to acquire Tecton for real-time features ([Perficient](https://blogs.perficient.com/2025/09/26/databricks-acquires-tecton/)). Design every vector index and online feature store as a rebuildable projection of lakehouse tables. Mind the defaults, too. Delta keeps deleted files for 7 days and logs for 30, which is too short for ML reproducibility, so set both to cover your lookback window ([Databricks VACUUM docs](https://docs.databricks.com/aws/sql/language-manual/delta-vacuum)).

Vector search trades recall for speed and memory. HNSW, an in-RAM proximity graph, is the low-latency default. IVF with product quantization (PQ) compresses vectors to cut memory. DiskANN moves most of the index to SSD: **it indexes a billion points on a 64 GB RAM workstation at over 95% recall@1 and under 5 ms latency** ([DiskANN paper](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~rak/papers/neurips19-diskann.pdf)). Products have split into four groups: cheap object-backed cold tiers, provisioned hot serving, vectors inside an existing database, and search platforms with built-in hybrid search and reranking. **Amazon S3 Vectors anchors the cold end: it went GA on 2 December 2025 with up to 2 billion vectors per index, warm queries of about 100 ms, and top-k capped at 100** ([AWS News Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/amazon-s3-vectors-now-generally-available-with-increased-scale-and-performance)). AWS positions OpenSearch as the hot tier in front of it ([S3 Vectors](https://aws.amazon.com/s3/features/vectors)). At the provisioned end, Pinecone made Dedicated Read Nodes GA on 15 April 2026 for steady workloads ([Pinecone](https://pinecone.io/newsroom/zoominfo-pinecone-drn/)). Inside Postgres, pgvector 0.8 added iterative index scans, which fix filtered HNSW queries that used to return fewer rows than the LIMIT ([pgvector guide](https://tessl.io/registry/skills/github/timescale/pg-aiguide/pgvector-semantic-search)). Among dedicated engines, Milvus 2.6 added hot/cold tiered storage and now writes directly to object storage ([BigDATAwire](https://www.bigdatawire.com/this-just-in/zilliz-releases-milvus-2-6-with-tiered-storage-and-int8-compression-to-cut-vector-search-costs)). Qdrant 1.16 added ACORN filtered search ([Qdrant](https://qdrant.tech/blog/qdrant-1.16.x/index.md)), and Elastic's DiskBBQ performs well up to about 95% recall ([Elastic](https://www.elastic.co/docs/reference/elasticsearch/mapping-reference/bbq)). Google's Vertex AI Vector Search 2.0 went GA on 5 March 2026 with hybrid search and built-in semantic reranking ([Vertex AI release notes](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/docs/release-notes)).

Filtered search is the most common production failure mode, so test with your real filter selectivity. The vendor benchmarks, including pgvectorscale's 2024 comparison with Pinecone, Pinecone's "97% lower costs" and AWS's "90%", are not comparable with each other ([TigerData](https://www.tigerdata.com/blog/pgvector-is-now-as-fast-as-pinecone-at-75-less-cost)). Run your own test of recall@k against p99 latency against cost. The standard retrieval stack runs BM25 and vector search in parallel, fuses the two lists with Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF, k ≈ 60), then reranks with a cross-encoder ([RRF paper](https://plg.uwaterloo.ca/~gvcormac/cormacksigir09-rrf.pdf)).

| Situation | Sensible vector store choice |
|---|---|
| Up to roughly 10–50M vectors, already on Postgres, need joins and filters | pgvector with halfvec and iterative scans; pgvectorscale or AlloyDB as you grow |
| Billions of vectors, low QPS, cost-dominated (archives, agent memory) | S3 Vectors, optionally fronted by OpenSearch |
| Spiky traffic, no ops team | Pinecone serverless, Zilliz Serverless or Qdrant Cloud |
| Steady high QPS | Provisioned capacity (Pinecone Dedicated Read Nodes, dedicated clusters) |
| Self-hosted at large scale; filter-heavy multi-tenant SaaS | Milvus; Qdrant |
| Data already in the lakehouse or warehouse | Databricks Vector Search, BigQuery `VECTOR_SEARCH` (batch), Snowflake Cortex Search |
| Single-cloud enterprise RAG needing hybrid search and reranking | Vertex AI Vector Search 2.0 or Azure AI Search |

Embedding storage is dimensions times bytes. **A float32 vector takes 4 × d bytes, so ten million 1536-dimension vectors take about 61 GB in float32, 15 GB in int8 and 1.9 GB in binary.** A neutral June 2026 benchmark measured average retrieval-quality drops of **1.95 points for int8 and 6.50 points for binary** ([HAKARI-Bench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.22778)). You can recover most of the binary loss by oversampling candidates and rescoring them with full-precision vectors. Matryoshka-style truncation compounds the savings: int8 at 256 dimensions takes 256 bytes per vector, against 2 KB for int8 at 2048 dimensions ([MongoDB/Voyage](https://www.mongodb.com/docs/voyageai/tutorials/dimensions-and-quantization/)). The working recipe starts with an embedding model trained with Matryoshka representation learning, so that its vectors can be truncated. Store the full-precision vectors cheaply, in object storage or a lakehouse column. Keep only a truncated, quantized copy in a RAM index. Oversample candidates 3–10×, rescore them with the full vectors, rerank, and validate recall on your own data. Changing embedding models means re-embedding everything, which is another reason to keep source text and chunk metadata in the lakehouse.

The remaining data concerns share one rule: keep every derived copy consistent with its source. Feature stores must enforce point-in-time correctness. Each training label joins only with feature values timestamped at or before the label's event time, which prevents label leakage and train/serve skew. Dataset versioning consolidated when lakeFS acquired DVC on 18 November 2025 ([lakeFS](https://lakefs.io/blog/lakefs-acquires-dvc/)). Pin every training run to a table snapshot ID. For streaming, the standard path runs change-data capture into Kafka, Kinesis or Pub/Sub. A Flink job then upserts into the vector DB or online store and also lands the data in Iceberg for training and rebuilds. The largest industry move here was IBM's agreement to buy Confluent for about $11B, announced 8 December 2025 ([MarketScreener](https://www.marketscreener.com/news/international-business-machines-corporation-entered-into-a-definitive-agreement-to-acquire-confluent-ce7d51d2d988f220)). Governance applies to derived data too. Embeddings and chunks are personal data if their source was, and retrieval needs per-user ACL filters. Deletion requests must reach snapshots, indexes and backups. Purging data from fine-tuned weights is practically infeasible, which is the strongest argument for never training on raw PII.

## The model layer: training and serving are data-movement problems

With hardware and data in place, the two core workloads behave the same way underneath. Training is limited by how much state fits and how fast gradients cross the network. Serving is limited by how many bytes each decode step must read.

### Training at scale: 16 bytes per parameter and an interruption every three hours

The ZeRO paper's memory ledger is still the standard reference. In mixed-precision Adam, each parameter needs 2 bytes for FP16/BF16 weights, 2 bytes for gradients, and 12 bytes for FP32 optimizer state (master weights, momentum and variance). That totals **16 bytes per parameter** before activations ([ZeRO, SC20](https://aiichironakano.github.io/cs596/Rajbhandari-ZeRO-SC20.pdf)). ZeRO-Infinity also counts an FP32 copy of the gradients and arrives at 20 bytes ([ZeRO-Infinity](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.07857)). At 16 bytes, a 7B model needs 112 GB and a 70B model 1.12 TB. ZeRO-3/FSDP sharding across 64 GPUs brings the 70B model down to 17.5 GB per GPU. For a 7.5B model across 64 data-parallel ranks, each ZeRO stage shrinks per-GPU state further: 120 GB unsharded, 31.4 GB with stage 1, 16.6 GB with stage 2 and 1.9 GB with stage 3.

Compute follows **training FLOPs ≈ 6 × parameters × tokens**. A 7B model on 2T tokens therefore needs 8.4 × 10²² FLOPs. At 40% model FLOPs utilization (MFU), that is about 59,000 H100-hours, or about 2.4 days on 1,024 GPUs. Meta's Llama 3 405B trained on up to 16K H100s ([Llama 3 paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.21783)). DeepSeek-V3 (671B total, 37B active parameters) trained on 14.8T tokens in **2.788M H800 GPU-hours, about $5.576M at an assumed $2/GPU-hour**. That figure covers the official run only, not ablations ([DeepSeek-V3 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.19437)). Applying 6ND gives about 330 TFLOP/s per GPU. That is roughly 33% of the H800's BF16 peak or 17% of its FP8 peak, so any MFU figure depends on which peak you divide by. TorchTitan's authors warn that MFU from FP8 runs is not comparable with BF16 MFU ([TorchTitan, ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/e6231c5f46598cfd09ff1970524e0436-Paper-Conference.pdf)). Activation memory dominates at long context. Recomputing activations instead of storing them raises compute from about 6ND to about 8ND ([Scaling Book: transformers](https://github.com/jax-ml/scaling-book/blob/main/transformers.md)). That increases hardware FLOPs utilization without increasing MFU.

Parallelism maps onto the network from the inside out. Tensor parallelism (with sequence parallelism) all-reduces activations several times per layer on the critical path, so it must stay on NVLink. On 8-GPU nodes that caps tensor parallelism at 8; NVL72 lets tensor and expert parallelism span 72 GPUs. Context and expert parallelism come next for long sequences and MoE models. Pipeline parallelism sends only boundary activations point to point and tolerates the scale-out network. Data parallelism with FSDP sharding goes outermost, because its gradient reduce-scatters and parameter all-gathers overlap with compute. Pipelines waste time in "bubbles." Under the common 1F1B schedule, the idle fraction is **(p−1)/(m+p−1)** for p stages and m micro-batches. That means 18% idle at 8 stages and 32 micro-batches. Interleaved schedules shrink the bubble by the number of virtual stages per GPU. Zero Bubble's ZB1P schedule cuts it to about a third of 1F1B's, and ZB2P approaches zero at about twice the activation memory ([Zero Bubble](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.10241)). DeepSeek's DualPipe also hides MoE all-to-all traffic behind computation ([DeepSeek-V3 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.19437)). FSDP2 shards each parameter individually using DTensor. Composing parallelism dimensions in TorchTitan beat optimized baselines by 65% for an 8B model on 128 GPUs, 12.6% for 70B on 256 GPUs and 30% for 405B on 512 GPUs ([TorchTitan](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/file/e6231c5f46598cfd09ff1970524e0436-Paper-Conference.pdf)). Megatron Bridge 0.6 (August 2026) highlights GLM-5.2 training at 128K context using context parallelism ([Megatron Bridge](https://github.com/NVIDIA-NeMo/Megatron-Bridge)). FSDP/ZeRO-3 moves roughly 50% more traffic than plain data parallelism. Hybrid sharding, which shards inside a node or rack and replicates across them, is the usual compromise on cloud networks.

The gap between scale-up and scale-out bandwidth is about 18–36×. NVLink 5 gives about 1.8 TB/s per GPU. Across nodes, every major cloud gives about **400 Gb/s (≈50 GB/s) per GPU**. Ring all-reduce moves 2(N−1)/N of the message per GPU regardless of GPU count, which makes it bandwidth-optimal for large messages; tree algorithms win on small, latency-bound ones. NCCL's defaults are not always right. On GB200 NVL72 partitions spanning racks, NCCL chose ring, yet tree reached **485.68 GB/s against ring's 393.66 GB/s at 24 GPUs** ([NVIDIA/nccl issue #1801](https://github.com/NVIDIA/nccl/issues/1801)). Each cloud reaches that ~400 Gb/s differently. AWS gives P5/P5en instances 3,200 Gbps of EFA, and P6e-GB200 UltraServers 28.8 Tb/s of EFAv4 in total ([AWS EC2 docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AWSEC2/latest/UserGuide/efa-acc-inst-types.html); [AWS P6](https://aws.amazon.com/ec2/instance-types/p6)). GCP gives A3 Ultra and A4 3.2 Tb/s of non-blocking RoCE with GPUDirect RDMA, and A3 Mega 1,800 Gbps via GPUDirect-TCPXO. **GPUDirect RDMA is incompatible with the TCPX and TCPXO plugins**, so don't install them on RDMA clusters ([GCP GPU network bandwidth](https://docs.cloud.google.com/compute/docs/gpus/gpu-network-bandwidth); [GKE GPUDirect-TCPX](https://docs.cloud.google.com/kubernetes-engine/docs/how-to/gpu-bandwidth-gpudirect-tcpx)). Azure uses InfiniBand. Each fabric needs its own NCCL plugin and environment settings, and getting them wrong is a common cause of slow jobs. The stakes are easy to compute. A 7B model's BF16 gradients total 14 GB, so a ring all-reduce moves about 28 GB per GPU per step. Without overlap, that takes 0.56 seconds at 50 GB/s, against 0.06 seconds over NVLink.

For numeric precision, **BF16 with FP32 master weights is the default**, and it needs no loss scaling. **FP8 is proven at frontier scale.** DeepSeek-V3 used fine-grained scaling, with 1×128 tiles for activations and 128×128 blocks for weights ([DeepSeek-V3 report](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.19437)). **4-bit training is now real.** NVIDIA's NVFP4 recipe trained a 12B hybrid Mamba-Transformer on 10T tokens to an MMLU-Pro score of 62.58%, against 62.62% in FP8. The recipe uses random Hadamard transforms, two-dimensional block quantization, stochastic rounding and a few layers kept in higher precision ([NVFP4 paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.25149)). Nemotron 3 used NVFP4 pretraining for up to 25T tokens ([Nemotron 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.20856)). NVIDIA also reports that the competing MXFP4 format needs 36% more tokens to reach the same loss ([NVIDIA Research](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/eai/blogs/pushing-intelligence-to-4-bit/)). Optimizer states usually stay in FP32, so lower precision mainly speeds up matrix multiplies rather than shrinking the 16-byte ledger. In practice, BF16 is the safe baseline and FP8 the mainstream speedup on Hopper and Blackwell. NVFP4 is the Blackwell frontier and is less battle-tested outside NVIDIA's own runs.

Reliability is now a design input. In a 54-day snapshot of Llama 3 training on 16K H100s, Meta recorded **466 job interruptions, 419 of them unexpected**. About 78% of the unexpected ones were confirmed or suspected hardware faults, with GPU issues alone accounting for 58.7%. The run still achieved **more than 90% effective training time**, and only three interruptions needed significant manual intervention ([Llama 3 paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.21783); [Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/tech-industry/artificial-intelligence/faulty-nvidia-h100-gpus-and-hbm3-memory-caused-half-of-the-failures-during-llama-3-training-one-failure-every-three-hours-for-metas-16384-gpu-training-cluster)). That is a mean time between failures (MTBF) of about 3.1 hours. If failures scale linearly with GPU count, a 100K-GPU job would see one about every 30 minutes. The **Young/Daly rule sets the checkpoint interval at W ≈ √(2 × MTBF × C)**, where C is the time to write a checkpoint ([Benoit et al.](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3549206.3549328)). The fraction of time wasted at that optimum is roughly √(2C/MTBF). With Meta's 186-minute MTBF, a 5-minute blocking checkpoint means checkpointing every 43 minutes and wasting about 23% of the run. A 6-second asynchronous checkpoint means every 6 minutes and about 3% waste.

The levers follow directly from the formula. Cut C with asynchronous distributed checkpointing. PyTorch reported more than 10× faster checkpointing, then about 19× with sub-second blocking once the GPU-to-CPU copy overlapped computation ([PyTorch blog](https://pytorch.org/blog/reducing-checkpointing-times/); [PyTorch forums](https://discuss.pytorch.org/t/distributed-w-torchtitan-optimizing-checkpointing-efficiency-with-pytorch-dcp/211250)). Watch for a reported race condition that can break asynchronous saves ([torchtitan #930](https://github.com/pytorch/torchtitan/issues/930)). Cut restart time with hot spares and automatic node replacement, cut lost work with in-memory replicas, and raise MTBF with burn-in and health checks. AWS productized several of these at once. **SageMaker HyperPod's checkpointless training (December 2025) recovers from the in-memory state of healthy accelerators.** AWS claims more than 95% goodput on clusters with thousands of accelerators and 80–93% lower recovery time. It pairs this with elastic training that scales jobs around higher-priority work ([AWS What's New](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2025/12/amazon-sagemaker-hyperpod-checkpointless-training); [AWS News Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/introducing-checkpointless-and-elastic-training-on-amazon-sagemaker-hyperpod)).

The framework landscape has settled into two families. The PyTorch-native family is FSDP2, DTensor, torchtitan and distributed checkpointing. The NVIDIA family is Megatron Core, packaged through Megatron Bridge (v0.6.0, August 2026, with two-way Hugging Face checkpoint conversion). NeMo AutoModel (v0.6.0) adds same-day fine-tuning support for Hugging Face models ([Megatron Bridge](https://github.com/NVIDIA-NeMo/Megatron-Bridge); [NeMo AutoModel](https://pypi.org/project/nemo-automodel/)). JAX with MaxText remains the TPU path. torchtune wound down active development in 2025 ([torchtune issue #2883](https://github.com/meta-pytorch/torchtune/issues/2883)).

For fine-tuning, **QLoRA fits a 65B model on a single 48 GB GPU, against more than 780 GB for 16-bit full fine-tuning**. It needs LoRA adapters on all linear layers to match full fine-tuning quality ([QLoRA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.14314)). Post-training now runs supervised fine-tuning (SFT), then DPO, then reinforcement learning (RL). GRPO, the common RL choice, drops PPO's learned critic and instead uses the mean reward of a group of sampled responses as its baseline ([DeepSeekMath](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.03300)). That makes RL at scale a distributed-systems problem, mixing vLLM-style generation with training.

Managed fine-tuning shifted sharply. **OpenAI is winding down self-serve fine-tuning.** The guide, updated 8 May 2026, says the platform no longer accepts new users. Existing customers can reportedly run jobs until 6 January 2027, and fine-tuned models keep serving until their base models are deprecated ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/model-optimization); [OpenAI community](https://community.openai.com/t/openai-s-self-serve-fine-tuning-availability/1380481)). Bedrock moved the other way. It launched reinforcement fine-tuning for Nova models in December 2025 and extended it in February 2026 to open-weight models, starting with qwen3-32b and gpt-oss-20b ([AWS What's New](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2026/02/amazon-bedrock-reinforcement-fine-tuning-openai/)). Fine-tuned models are not portable across platforms, so the choice of platform locks you in.

For experiment tracking, **Weights & Biases has belonged to CoreWeave since 5 May 2025 (about $1.7B)**. Since 30 September 2026, users sign in through CoreWeave Forge, with existing contracts unchanged ([CoreWeave](https://www.coreweave.com/news/coreweave-completes-acquisition-of-weights-biases-2); [CoreWeave W&B page](https://www.coreweave.com/companies/weights-and-biases)). For large runs, log MFU per step, per-rank step time to catch stragglers, checkpoint durations, and restart events so you can compute goodput.

### Inference and serving: decode is bandwidth-bound, so batching, KV reuse and disaggregation win

LLM inference has two phases with opposite bottlenecks. Prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel. It is compute-bound and sets time to first token (TTFT). Decode emits one token per step and must re-read every weight plus the KV cache each time. It is memory-bandwidth-bound and sets time per output token (TPOT), also called inter-token latency (ITL). At batch size 1, decode matrix multiplies run at about 1% of peak FLOPS ([Baseten Inference Engineering](https://www.baseten.co/inference-engineering/book/02-models/calculating-inference-bottlenecks/)). The H100's roofline ridge, 989 TFLOPS of BF16 over 3.35 TB/s, sits at about 295 FLOPs per byte, and batching raises decode intensity roughly linearly. A worked bound makes this concrete. A 70B FP8 model on one H100 needs at least 21 ms per decode step (70 GB ÷ 3.35 TB/s), capping a single stream near 48 tokens/s. At batch 64, the same weight read serves 64 streams, approaching 3,000 tokens/s in aggregate at nearly the same per-user TPOT. Batching therefore adds throughput almost for free until the GPU becomes compute-bound or runs out of KV memory.

The serving metric that matters is **goodput: the request rate that still meets both TTFT and TPOT targets**. DistServe, which defined it, served 7.4× more requests or met 12.6× tighter latency targets than the systems it compared against ([DistServe, OSDI '24](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi24/presentation/zhong-yinmin)). Report p50, p90 and p99 TTFT and ITL alongside throughput, because throughput can always be bought with tail latency. In MLPerf Inference v6.0 (April 2026), NVIDIA reported a GB300 NVL72 rack at 575,580 tokens/s on DeepSeek-R1 in the Server scenario. AMD reported an 87-GPU MI355X system at 1.04M tokens/s Offline on Llama 2 70B. Both figures are vendor-reported ([NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/nvidia-extreme-co-design-delivers-new-mlperf-inference-records/); [AMD](https://www.amd.com/en/blogs/2026/amd-delivers-breakthrough-mlperf-inference-6-0-results.html)).

KV cache usually decides how many sequences fit. For a Llama-3-70B-class model with grouped-query attention (80 layers, 8 KV heads, head dimension 128), the arithmetic works out as follows:

| KV format | Per token | 8K-token sequence | 128K-token sequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| BF16 | ≈320 KiB | ≈2.5 GiB | ≈40 GiB |
| FP8 | ≈160 KiB | ≈1.25 GiB | ≈20 GiB |
| NVFP4 (~4.5 bits including scales) | ≈90 KiB | ≈0.7 GiB | ≈11 GiB |
| Same model with full multi-head attention (64 KV heads), BF16 | ≈2.5 MiB | ≈20 GiB | ≈320 GiB |

Four lines of work shrink or stretch the cache. The first makes KV smaller per token. Grouped-query and multi-query attention share KV heads across query heads. DeepSeek-V2's multi-head latent attention cut KV cache by 93.3% relative to DeepSeek 67B and raised maximum throughput 5.76× ([DeepSeek-V2](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.04434v2)). NVFP4 KV cache is up to 50% smaller than FP8 with under 1% accuracy loss, and FP8 KV is already the production baseline ([NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/optimizing-inference-for-long-context-and-large-batch-sizes-with-nvfp4-kv-cache/)). The second removes fragmentation: PagedAttention delivered 2–4× throughput at the same latency ([vLLM paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180)). The third reuses KV across requests. Prefix caching is on by default in vLLM's V1 engine ([vLLM Ascend release notes](https://docs.vllm.ai/projects/ascend/en/latest/user_guide/release_notes.html)), and SGLang's RadixAttention does the same. The fourth stretches capacity through tiered offload. LMCache, presented at MLSys 2026, claims up to 15× throughput on multi-round QA. Its CacheBlend technique reuses KV even for RAG chunks that are not prefixes ([MLSys 2026: LMCache](https://mlsys.org/virtual/2026/oral/3646)). NVIDIA Dynamo's KV Block Manager offloads to CPU memory, SSD and network storage, and supports vLLM and TensorRT-LLM but not SGLang ([Dynamo KVBM](https://docs.nvidia.com/dynamo/components/kvbm)). On two 80 GB GPUs serving 70B FP8 weights, roughly 70–80 GB remains for KV. That holds about 30 concurrent 8K sequences with BF16 KV, and FP8 KV doubles it. Prefix caching pays only when shared content (system prompt, tools, documents) comes first and is byte-identical.

Scheduling evolved in stages. Static batching gave way to continuous, iteration-level batching, then to chunked prefill, and now to disaggregation. Sarathi-Serve's chunked prefill stops long prompts from stalling ongoing decodes. It reported up to 2.6× capacity within latency targets versus vLLM, and up to 5.6× for Falcon-180B with pipeline parallelism ([Sarathi-Serve](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.02310)). **Disaggregated serving runs prefill and decode on separate GPU pools linked by fast KV transfer.** NVIDIA Dynamo 1.0, GA on 16 March 2026, packages disaggregated serving, a KV-aware router, an autoscaling planner, the KV Block Manager and the NIXL transfer library. NVIDIA claims up to 7× on Blackwell ([NVIDIA Newsroom](https://nvidianews.nvidia.com/news/dynamo-1-0)), and documentation exists through v1.3.0 ([Dynamo docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/dynamo/v1.3.0/kubernetes-deployment/request-routing/gateway-api-inference-extension/overview.md)). SGLang with disaggregation and large-scale expert parallelism reached about 52.3k input and 22.3k output tokens/s per node across 96 H100s on DeepSeek ([LMSYS](https://lmsys.org/blog/2025-05-05-large-scale-ep/)). llm-d has been a CNCF Sandbox project since March 2026 ([llm-d](https://github.com/llm-d/llm-d)). Mixing chip types is now real: Cerebras's SEC filing describes AWS pairing Trainium3 for prefill with Cerebras CS-3 for decode ([Cerebras 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0002021728/000162828026044941/cbrsannouncesfinancialresu.htm)). Disaggregation pays off for long prompts, strict ITL targets, MoE models needing wide expert parallelism, and high scale. In small deployments, KV-transfer overhead and fixed pool ratios hurt, and aggregated serving with chunked prefill stays simpler.

Open serving is effectively a three-engine world. vLLM (0.31.0 on 5 October 2026, released roughly every nine days) has the broadest hardware and model coverage ([PyPI: vllm](https://pypi.org/project/vllm/)). SGLang leads on prefix-heavy, agentic and RL-rollout workloads and on large MoE expert parallelism. **TensorRT-LLM removed its TensorRT engine backend**; PyTorch is now the only execution backend, and `trtllm-serve <hf_model>` needs no engine-build step ([TensorRT-LLM migration guide](https://nvidia.github.io/TensorRT-LLM/legacy/tensorrt-backend-removal.html)). **Hugging Face TGI is in maintenance mode**, and Hugging Face itself recommends vLLM, SGLang, llama.cpp and MLX instead ([TGI README](https://trendshift.io/admin/repository/ask-ai/4188)). KServe became a CNCF incubating project in September 2025 and integrates llm-d through `LLMInferenceService` ([CNCF](https://www.cncf.io/blog/2025/11/11/kserve-becomes-a-cncf-incubating-project/)). Triton remains relevant for non-LLM ensembles. Ray Serve and BentoML handle application-level composition, and llama.cpp and Ollama cover local, low-concurrency use.

The 2026 optimization defaults start with quantization. FP8 weights and activations are standard. NVFP4 is the Blackwell 4-bit format, and NVIDIA reports at most 1% accuracy degradation when quantizing DeepSeek-R1 to it after training, a vendor claim ([NVIDIA Technical Blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/?p=102000)). INT4 weight-only methods such as AWQ and GPTQ remain useful on older GPUs. For attention kernels, **FlashAttention-4 (MLSys 2026) reaches up to 1,605 TFLOP/s on B200 in BF16, about 71% utilization.** That is 1.3× faster than cuDNN 9.13 and 2.7× faster than Triton, partly because it computes the exponential function with polynomial approximations on the FMA units ([Tri Dao](https://tridao.me/blog/2026/flash4/)). Speculative decoding has the most nuanced payoff. Its gains depend on α, the probability that the target model accepts a drafted token, and γ, the number of tokens drafted per step. Expected tokens per target pass equal (1−α^(γ+1))/(1−α), which gives 2.94 at α = 0.7 and γ = 5. EAGLE-3 draft heads deliver about 2× on Llama-3.1-8B in vLLM and SGLang ([BentoML](https://bentoml.com/blog/3x-faster-llm-inference-with-speculative-decoding)). Results vary sharply by workload, though: one test measured 1.84× on code refactoring but **0.99× on general chat** ([mohakchugh](https://mohakchugh.is-a.dev/blog/eagle-3-speculative-decoding-training-time-test)). Below α ≈ 0.55, speculation is a net loss ([PremAI](https://blog.premai.io/speculative-decoding-2-3x-faster-llm-inference-2026)). Speculation spends spare compute to save bandwidth-bound steps, and that spare compute disappears at high batch sizes. Enable it for low-latency tiers, and use n-gram drafting for RAG and code edits, where outputs copy from the input.

Production deployment on Kubernetes has converged on a few patterns. **The Gateway API Inference Extension's InferencePool is at v1 and generally available.** Its endpoint picker chooses replicas by KV-cache utilization and queue length ([GAIE InferencePool](https://gateway-api-inference-extension.sigs.k8s.io/api-types/inferencepool/)), so prefix-aware, load-aware routing is the main win over round-robin. Autoscale on queue depth, KV utilization and TTFT/ITL against targets, never on GPU utilization, which reads near 100% even under light decode load. Weight loading is the cold-start bottleneck. The Run:ai Model Streamer reads safetensors files concurrently from SSD, network file systems or S3 ([NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/reducing-cold-start-latency-for-llm-inference-with-nvidia-runai-model-streamer)). Combined with Lustre striping, it cut one 554 GiB load across 16 tensor-parallel GPUs from about 76 minutes to about 5 ([Dynamo fast model loading](https://docs.dynamo.nvidia.com/dynamo/kubernetes/model-deployment/model-loading/fast-model-loading)). S-LoRA serves thousands of LoRA adapters over shared base weights, with up to 4× the throughput of naive LoRA serving in vLLM ([S-LoRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03285v2)). Multi-region options differ across managed APIs. Azure offers Global, Data Zone and Regional deployments, plus spillover from provisioned to standard capacity ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-foundry/openai/concepts/provisioned-throughput)). Vertex AI provisioned throughput is bound to project, region and model ([Vertex AI docs](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/provisioned-throughput/measure-provisioned-throughput?hl=en)). Prompt caches are scoped to model and region, so failover costs cache hits.

Managed model APIs now share four levers: on-demand per-token pricing, asynchronous batch at about 50% off within 24 hours, priority or provisioned capacity, and prompt caching with cache reads at about 10% of the input price or less. OpenAI's Flex tier is synchronous at batch rates but may return 429 errors when capacity is short ([OpenAI Flex processing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/flex-processing)). Vertex enables implicit caching by default at a 90% discount, but its batch and cache discounts do not stack ([Vertex context caching](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/context-cache/context-cache-overview)). Anthropic's cache multipliers do stack with batch ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). Among specialist providers, **NVIDIA licensed Groq's inference technology non-exclusively for about $20B in December 2025.** Groq's founders moved to NVIDIA and Groq continues independently, and a stockholder suit in October 2026 alleges a "lowball" price ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/12/24/nvidia-buying-ai-chip-startup-groq-for-about-20-billion-biggest-deal.html); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/05/nvidia-groq-deal-stockholder-lawsuit.html)). Artificial Analysis figures, reported secondhand, put Cerebras at about 1,700 tokens/s on gpt-oss-120B, against about 690 for Fireworks and 564 for Together AI ([fast.io](https://fast.io/resources/fastest-ai-2026/)). Speeds on a single model ranged from 92 to 1,916 tokens/s across hosts in one high-reasoning test, so always quote speed with the model, date and reasoning mode.

## The application layer: tokens, traces and tools replace weights and features

Most AI engineers now ship applications on models they did not train. That changes what gets versioned, tested and monitored.

### MLOps and LLMOps: the registry now versions prompts, judges and MCP servers

Google's three-level maturity model is still the reference. Level 0 is manual, notebook-driven work. Level 1 automates continuous training, with data and model validation, a feature store, metadata tracking and triggers. Level 2 adds CI/CD for the pipeline itself plus a model registry ([Google Cloud MLOps guide](https://cloud.google.com/architecture/mlops-continuous-delivery-and-automation-pipelines-in-machine-learning)). LLMOps changes the contents of each level. The versioned artifacts become prompts, model and provider configurations, tool and MCP definitions, retrieval indexes and agent harness code, often with no model weights at all. The quality signal shifts from accuracy to eval suites, and the unit of monitoring becomes the trace. Cost per token becomes a service-level objective, and continuous training gives way to continuous evaluation and prompt optimization. MLflow shows the shift. **MLflow 3 (11 June 2025) rebuilt itself around tracing** ([MLflow blog](https://www.mlflow.org/blog/mlflow-3-launch)). By 3.17.1 (13 October 2026) it versions prompts, evaluation datasets, judges and MCP servers (an MCP Registry arrived in 3.15). It also runs an AI Gateway with traffic splits, fallbacks, budgets and guardrails. Version 3.16 made authorization fail-closed by default and removed the default admin password, so review the auth setup on any self-hosted server upgraded from older versions ([MLflow changelog](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mlflow/mlflow/master/CHANGELOG.md)). Google folded Vertex AI into the **Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform** on 22 April 2026 ([HPCwire](https://www.hpcwire.com/aiwire/2026/04/23/google-unveils-gemini-enterprise-agent-platform/)). The vendor field consolidated fast. ClickHouse acquired Langfuse (16 January 2026) ([ClickHouse](https://clickhouse.com/blog/clickhouse-acquires-langfuse-open-source-llm-observability)). OpenAI announced it would acquire Promptfoo (9 March 2026) ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/blog/openai-to-acquire-promptfoo)). Mintlify bought Helicone, which went into maintenance mode ([Helicone](https://helicone.ai/blog/joining-mintlify)), and Apple acquired WhyLabs and discontinued its commercial platform ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2025/founders-at-seattle-startup-whylabs-join-apple-following-under-the-radar-acquisition)). That churn is the strongest argument for instrumenting with open standards.

CI for ML now has three test layers. The first is ordinary code tests. The second is data and model tests, such as Evidently Test Suites with pass/fail conditions ([Evidently](https://github.com/evidentlyai/evidently)). The third is LLM eval suites run as tests: DeepEval's pytest-style `deepeval test run` ([DeepEval](https://github.com/confident-ai/deepeval)), promptfoo ([promptfoo](https://github.com/promptfoo/promptfoo)) and MLflow's `@mlflow.test`, added in 3.14. The older Iterative tooling has faded; **CML's last release was v0.20.6 in October 2024** ([CML releases](https://github.com/iterative/cml/releases)). Anthropic's guidance separates two kinds of eval. Capability evals should start at a low pass rate, "a hill to climb." Regression evals should sit near 100%. Saturated capability evals "graduate" into the regression suite ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)).

Infrastructure as code has diverged into two camps. Terraform is at 1.16.6, and the 1.17 release candidate makes Terraform Policy GA ([Terraform releases](https://github.com/hashicorp/terraform/releases)). OpenTofu 1.13.1 has been shipping features such as state encryption and ephemeral resources first. Ephemeral resources keep model-provider API keys out of state files ([OpenTofu releases](https://github.com/opentofu/opentofu/releases)). On the Kubernetes side, the current versions are Crossplane 2.4 ([Crossplane](https://github.com/crossplane/crossplane/releases)) and Argo CD 3.5.5, whose 3.6 release candidates fix four critical security issues ([Argo CD](https://github.com/argoproj/argo-cd/releases)). Flux is at 2.9.6 ([Flux](https://github.com/fluxcd/flux2/releases)). The typical layering puts Terraform in charge of the VPC, cluster, GPU node pools, IAM and buckets. GitOps reconciles the GPU Operator, the serving stack and the telemetry collectors. Argo Rollouts handles canary releases of model servers.

Deployment strategies differ by platform. SageMaker deployment guardrails implement blue/green deployments with all-at-once, canary or linear traffic shifting. Each step has a "baking period," and CloudWatch alarms trigger automatic rollback. Both fleets are billed throughout the update ([AWS docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/sagemaker/latest/dg/deployment-guardrails-blue-green.html)). Argo Rollouts provides canary and blue/green releases with metric analysis, and its traffic mirroring supports shadow deployments ([Argo Rollouts](https://github.com/argoproj/argo-rollouts)). For LLM apps, the unit of release is usually a prompt-plus-model-plus-parameters configuration, rolled out like a feature flag. LaunchDarkly's AI Configs guarded rollouts revert automatically when guardrail metrics regress ([LaunchDarkly](https://launchdarkly.com/changelog/guarded-rollouts-for-ai-configs/)). Rollback triggers must include quality signals such as online judge scores and thumbs-down rates, not just errors and latency.

LLM observability has converged on **OpenTelemetry's GenAI semantic conventions**. All of them are still "Development" status, and in June 2026 they moved to a dedicated repository, semantic-conventions-genai ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/azena-ai/opentelemetrys-genai-semantic-conventions-are-not-stable-yet-heres-what-actually-shipped-in-2026-3mke); [semantic-conventions-genai](https://github.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai)). They name operations such as chat, embeddings, retrieval, `execute_tool`, `invoke_agent` and memory operations. They define attributes such as `gen_ai.provider.name` and `gen_ai.usage.input_tokens`, which includes cached tokens. Capturing prompt and response content is opt-in and off by default ([gen-ai-spans](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/docs/gen-ai/gen-ai-spans.md)). The conventions also specify server metrics for time to first token and time per output token ([gen-ai-metrics](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/docs/gen-ai/gen-ai-metrics.md)). Token counters report cache reads and reasoning tokens separately ([gen-ai-token-metrics](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/docs/gen-ai/gen-ai-token-metrics.md)), and a `gen_ai.evaluation.result` event carries judge scores. Datadog has ingested OTel GenAI spans natively since December 2025 ([Datadog](https://datadoghq.com/blog/llm-otel-semantic-convention/)). Self-hostable backends include Langfuse (MIT-licensed core, built on ClickHouse) and Arize Phoenix (Elastic License 2.0) ([Langfuse](https://github.com/langfuse/langfuse); [Phoenix](https://github.com/Arize-ai/phoenix)). Because renames are still happening, pin instrumentation library versions and expect dashboards to break on upgrades. Track TTFT and TPOT, tokens by type, cost per request, user and feature, error types and fallbacks, tool calls per agent invocation (to detect loops), online judge scores and the empty-retrieval rate. Classic drift statistics such as PSI and KS still apply to tabular models.

Evaluation practice has a clear consensus. Anthropic's January 2026 guide treats the harness plus the model as the unit under test. It recommends starting with 20–50 tasks drawn from real failures and grading outcomes rather than paths. It also distinguishes **pass@k (at least one success in k trials) from pass^k (all k succeed)**. A 75% per-trial success rate yields only about 42% across three trials, which is the number that matters for customer-facing consistency ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). Hamel Husain's methodology adds five rules. Use binary pass/fail verdicts instead of Likert scales, with one failure mode per judge. Exhaust code-based checks before writing judges. Read about 100 traces before the failure taxonomy saturates. Calibrate each judge against human labels using true- and false-positive rates ([evals-skills](https://github.com/hamelsmu/evals-skills); [skill mirror](https://tessl.io/registry/skills/github/hamelsmu/evals-skills/eval-audit)). LLM judges show position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases. GPT-4 agreed with humans more than 80% of the time, but its verdicts stayed consistent when answer order was swapped only 65% of the time ([Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)). So swap answer orders and use a judge from a different model family than the generator. For RAG, the RAGAS metrics separate the retriever from the generator. Faithfulness is the fraction of response claims supported by the retrieved context. Context precision@K rewards ranking relevant chunks first. Context recall needs a reference answer ([RAGAS faithfulness](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/vibrantlabsai/ragas/main/docs/concepts/metrics/available_metrics/faithfulness.md); [RAGAS context precision](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/vibrantlabsai/ragas/main/docs/concepts/metrics/available_metrics/context_precision.md)). The UK AI Security Institute's Inspect framework ships more than 200 prebuilt evaluations ([Inspect](https://github.com/UKGovernmentBEIS/inspect_ai)).

Workflow orchestration has split into two tiers. Offline batch work (eval runs, index rebuilds, fine-tunes) runs on schedulers such as Airflow, Dagster, Prefect or Kubeflow Pipelines. Airflow 3.3.2 brings asset partitioning, a state store and human-in-the-loop tasks ([Airflow](https://github.com/apache/airflow/releases)). Long-running agents run on durable-execution engines such as Temporal, whose OpenAI Agents SDK integration is still in public preview and was extended to sandboxes in April 2026 ([Temporal](https://temporal.io/blog/announcing-openai-agents-sdk-integration); [Temporal changelog](https://temporal.io/change-log/product-area/python-sdk)).

### GenAI application architecture: hybrid retrieval, durable agents and a newly stateless MCP

Production RAG in 2026 is a multi-stage pipeline. A layout-aware parser extracts each document, and the text is split into chunks enriched with document-level context. The chunks are embedded and also indexed for BM25 keyword search. At query time, both retrievers run and their results are fused with RRF, a cross-encoder reranks the merged list, and the model generates a grounded answer with citations. Anthropic's Contextual Retrieval study is still the most-cited evidence. It prepends a 50–100-token, chunk-specific context to each chunk before embedding and BM25 indexing. That cut top-20 retrieval failures by **35% (from 5.7% to 3.7%)**. Adding contextual BM25 brought the cut to **49%**, and adding reranking (retrieving 150 candidates and keeping 20) to **67%**, or a 1.9% failure rate. With prompt caching, the one-time cost was about $1.02 per million document tokens. The same study advises skipping RAG entirely for knowledge bases under about 200,000 tokens (roughly 500 pages) and putting them in a cached prompt instead ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/contextual-retrieval)). Large, changing, permissioned or multi-tenant corpora still need RAG. Per-query cost grows linearly with context, and accuracy degrades for information buried mid-context ([Lost in the Middle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)). Barnett et al. catalog seven RAG failure points, from missing content to incomplete answers ([arXiv 2401.05856](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05856)). GraphRAG earns its indexing cost for global, thematic questions ([GraphRAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16130)). LazyGraphRAG cuts indexing cost to about 0.1% of full GraphRAG ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/blog/lazygraphrag-setting-a-new-standard-for-quality-and-cost/)). Managed services wrap the same chain: Bedrock Knowledge Bases, RAG Engine inside Google's Agent Platform, and Azure's Foundry IQ. Foundry IQ breaks complex queries into parallel subqueries and enforces ACLs under the caller's Entra identity. It also **exposes knowledge bases as MCP endpoints** ([Microsoft Foundry devblog](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/foundry/foundry-iq-agent-framework-integration/)). Choose the service by where your data and identities already live.

On agents, Anthropic's December 2024 taxonomy remains the shared vocabulary. Workflows follow predefined code paths: prompt chaining, routing, parallelization, orchestrator-workers and evaluator-optimizer. Agents direct their own tool use. The advice is to start from raw APIs and to design tools that are hard to misuse ([Anthropic: Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). In Anthropic's multi-agent research system, a lead agent spawns parallel subagents, and the system beat a single agent by 90.2% on an internal eval. The cost is tokens: agents use about 4× the tokens of chat, and **multi-agent systems about 15×**. Token usage alone explained 80% of performance variance on BrowseComp ([Anthropic: multi-agent research system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Treat context as a budget. Presenting MCP tools as code APIs that the agent calls from a sandbox cut one Drive-to-Salesforce workflow from about 150,000 tokens to 2,000 (−98.7%) ([Anthropic: code execution with MCP](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/code-execution-with-mcp)). Human-in-the-loop approval belongs in the runtime, not the prompt: pre-tool hooks and permission callbacks such as the Claude Agent SDK's `PreToolUse` and `can_use_tool` ([Claude Agent SDK](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-agent-sdk-python)), or LangGraph interrupts ([LangGraph](https://github.com/langchain-ai/langgraph)).

The framework layer has consolidated around a handful of code-first SDKs. The Claude Agent SDK packages the Claude Code harness as a library with in-process MCP tool servers ([Claude Agent SDK](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-agent-sdk-python)). The OpenAI Agents SDK provides handoffs, guardrails, sessions and sandbox agents, and works with other providers' models ([OpenAI Agents SDK](https://github.com/openai/openai-agents-python)). LangGraph offers durable, checkpointed graphs. Microsoft Agent Framework succeeds Semantic Kernel and AutoGen, with 1.0 GA reported for 3 April 2026 by secondary sources ([Microsoft Agent Framework](https://github.com/microsoft/agent-framework); [Atlan](https://atlan.com/know/ai-agent/microsoft/semantic-kernel/)). Google ADK rounds out the group ([ADK](https://github.com/google/adk-python)). One legacy path to retire: **OpenAI's Assistants API was scheduled to shut down on 26 August 2026**, replaced by the Responses and Conversations APIs ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/mr_manushukla/openai-assistants-api-shuts-down-26-august-2026-a-3-week-migration-sprint-plan-596b)). Among managed runtimes, Bedrock AgentCore bundles a serverless runtime, a gateway that turns APIs into MCP tools, identity, memory, a code interpreter, a browser, OpenTelemetry observability, evaluations and Cedar-based policy ([AgentCore samples](https://github.com/awslabs/amazon-bedrock-agentcore-samples)). Google's Agent Engine sits inside the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform. Microsoft Foundry Agent Service reportedly made hosted agents GA on 9 July 2026 ([tech-insider.org](https://tech-insider.org/aws-bedrock-agentcore-vs-azure-ai-foundry-vs-vertex-ai-agent-builder-2026/)). The same secondary source puts AgentCore and Agent Engine runtime compute prices within about 4% of each other, so the runtimes now differentiate on identity, policy, evals and data integration.

The protocol layer matured. MCP, introduced by Anthropic in November 2024, now sits under the Linux Foundation's **Agentic AI Foundation, formed 9 December 2025** ([WorkOS](https://workos.com/blog/the-linux-foundation-launches-the-aaif)). **The 2026-07-28 MCP revision made the protocol stateless.** It retired the `initialize` handshake and the `Mcp-Session-Id` header. Every request now carries its own protocol version, client identity and capabilities, so any request can reach any server instance behind a plain round-robin load balancer. Multi Round-Trip Requests replace server-initiated sampling and elicitation. Required `Mcp-Method` and `Mcp-Name` headers let gateways and web application firewalls route and meter traffic without parsing request bodies. List results become cacheable and deterministically ordered, which keeps upstream prompt caches stable. Dynamic client registration is deprecated in favor of Client ID Metadata Documents. Roots, Sampling and Logging are deprecated under a formal 12-month minimum deprecation policy ([MCP blog](https://blog.modelcontextprotocol.io/posts/2026-07-28/)). The tier-1 SDKs see about half a billion downloads a month. The earlier 2025-11-25 revision added experimental durable Tasks and URL-mode elicitation ([MCP changelog](https://github.com/modelcontextprotocol/modelcontextprotocol/blob/main/docs/specification/2025-11-25/changelog.mdx)). **A2A, the agent-to-agent protocol Google introduced, reached v1.0 on 12 March 2026.** It adds a `tasks/list` method, PKCE and device-code OAuth flows, and gRPC multi-tenancy ([A2A releases](https://github.com/a2aproject/A2A/releases)). MCP connects agents to tools; A2A connects agents to agents. The engineering consequence is that MCP servers now deploy as ordinary stateless HTTP services on Lambda, Cloud Run or Azure Functions, with per-tool authorization at the gateway. Servers that relied on sessions need refactoring.

Long-running agents deploy as durable workflows. The API accepts a task, returns HTTP 202 with a task ID, and enqueues the work. A workflow engine (Temporal, Step Functions, Durable Functions or LangGraph) then drives the agent loop, running each model or tool call as a retried activity. Checkpoints land in Postgres, DynamoDB, Cosmos DB or Firestore, and progress streams back over server-sent events (SSE) or webhooks.
Agent-generated code needs isolation. Firecracker microVMs, used by E2B and AgentCore's code interpreter, enforce a hardware virtualization boundary. That is stronger than gVisor's syscall interception, which Modal uses and which supports GPUs ([Northflank](https://northflank.com/blog/e2b-vs-modal)). Firecracker boots in about 125 ms with under 5 MiB of memory overhead per microVM ([Firecracker, NSDI 2020](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi20/presentation/agache)). The rule follows: microVMs for untrusted code, gVisor for semi-trusted GPU work, and containers only for trusted code.

Caching is the single biggest application-level cost lever, and the providers implement it differently.

| Provider | Mechanism | Cache-hit price | Retention |
|---|---|---|---|
| Anthropic | Explicit breakpoints or automatic caching; writes cost 1.25× (5-minute) or 2× (1-hour) base input | 0.1× input; 0.05× on Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5; 0.025× on Fable 5.1 | 5 minutes by default, refreshed on each hit; 1-hour option |
| OpenAI | Automatic prefix caching at 1,024+ tokens | ~10% of input on newer models | 24-hour extended option up to GPT-5.5; on GPT-5.6+, a 30-minute TTL and billed cache writes |
| Google Gemini | Implicit (free) or explicit (billed for storage) | 90% off on Gemini 2.5+ | Configurable for explicit caches |

Sources: [Anthropic prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching); [OpenAI prompt caching](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-caching); [Microsoft Foundry docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-my/Azure/foundry/openai/how-to/prompt-caching); [Vertex context caching](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/context-cache/context-cache-overview).

On Anthropic, invalidation follows a hierarchy: changing the tool definitions invalidates everything after them. Prompts below the model's minimum cacheable length (512–4,096 tokens depending on the model) silently don't cache. Lay prompts out with static tools first, then the system prompt, then stable documents, then the conversation, and volatile content last. Never put a timestamp near the top. Semantic caching is a separate, lossy layer that produces false positives ([GPTCache](https://github.com/zilliztech/GPTCache)). Never apply it to answers that depend on user-specific context.

Production stacks also put an AI gateway between applications and providers. LiteLLM supports 100+ providers, virtual keys and spend tracking. It claims 8 ms p95 overhead at 1,000 requests per second, though a third party says it degrades near 2,000 ([LiteLLM](https://github.com/BerriAI/litellm); [Cloudnuro](https://www.cloudnuro.ai/blog/ai-gateway-buyers-guide-2026-kong-vs-portkey-vs-litellm-vs-truefoundry)). Envoy AI Gateway, renamed "Agent Router," is now an Agentic AI Foundation project ([Envoy AI Gateway](https://github.com/envoyproxy/ai-gateway)). Kong and Azure API Management also serve as AI gateways, with APIM adding token limits, semantic caching and MCP support ([Azure AI-Gateway samples](https://github.com/Azure-Samples/AI-Gateway)). Client-side reliability is classic distributed-systems practice. Retry 429 and 5xx responses with capped exponential backoff and full jitter, honoring `Retry-After` ([AWS Architecture Blog](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/architecture/exponential-backoff-and-jitter/)). Put a circuit breaker on each backend. Fall back to the same model in another region before switching models, because a model switch changes behavior and loses the cache. Constrain outputs with schemas. When OpenAI launched structured outputs, its newest model scored 100% schema adherence on OpenAI's eval, against under 40% for an older model ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-structured-outputs-in-the-api/)).

All three clouds converge on the same logical stack. Microsoft's "Baseline Microsoft Foundry chat" reference architecture is the most explicit published blueprint. A chat front end runs on App Service behind Application Gateway with a WAF. Foundry Agent Service runs on customer-owned Cosmos DB, AI Search and Storage. Private endpoints are used throughout, and all egress is forced through Azure Firewall ([Azure Architecture Center](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/ai-ml/architecture/baseline-microsoft-foundry-chat)). ## The control layer: security is architectural and cost is utilization

Security and cost decide whether a system survives production. Both are being reshaped by the same fact: the unit of risk and spend is no longer the model but the system around it.

### Security and governance: guardrails filter, architecture defends, and the EU clock is running

Cloud security for AI stacks five controls: **identity → network → encryption → logging → application authorization**. The cloud provider supplies the first four. The fifth is yours to build, and it is where most LLM-specific incidents happen. Keyless workload identity through OIDC federation works the same way on EKS, GKE and AKS ([jorijn.com](https://jorijn.com/en/knowledge-base/kubernetes/security/workload-identity-irsa-eks-gke-aks/)). AWS recommends EKS Pod Identity over IRSA wherever it is supported ([Hidekazu Konishi](https://hidekazu-konishi.com/entry/amazon_eks_pod_identity_and_irsa_decision_guide.html)). Bedrock supports customer-managed KMS keys and private connectivity through PrivateLink ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/bedrock/security-privacy-responsible-ai)). Its model invocation logging, which records prompts and responses, is off by default and separate from CloudTrail. Once enabled, those logs are a sensitive data store in their own right ([Bedrock docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/model-invocation-logging.html)). One vendor calls the remaining weakness the "request gap": guardrails, PrivateLink and IAM each do a different job, and none of them decides whether a specific authenticated caller should be allowed to send a given prompt ([DeepInspect](https://www.deepinspect.ai/blog/aws-bedrock-security)).

On data protection, the hyperscalers' commitment not to train on customer API data is settled ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/bedrock/security-privacy-responsible-ai)). Retention is the open question, because **zero data retention (ZDR) is the default nowhere**. Azure grants ZDR only through an approved Modified Abuse Monitoring exemption, typically for enterprise-agreement customers. Even then it covers only abuse-monitoring storage. The Responses API, stored completions, files and fine-tuning each keep their own retention ([Microsoft Q&A](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-au/answers/questions/5954113/modified-abuse-monitoring-on-azure-foundry)). Vertex AI caches Gemini inputs and outputs in memory for up to 24 hours per project by default, and you must disable that to reach ZDR. Grounding with Google Search keeps logs for up to three days ([Google Cloud ZDR](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/vertex-ai-zero-data-retention)). Check each feature you enable, including cross-region inference profiles, which can route traffic out of a geography. Confidential GPU computing exists but has limits. Azure's NCC H100 v5 VMs pair AMD SEV-SNP CPUs with H100s in confidential mode ([Azure](https://azure.microsoft.com/en-us/blog/how-azure-is-ensuring-the-future-of-gpus-is-confidential/)). Google offers Confidential Space with Hopper GPUs and Blackwell-based G4 confidential VMs ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/identity-security/verifiable-trust-in-the-ai-era-whats-new-in-confidential-computing)). A 2025 analysis found multi-GPU confidential computing disabled and only limited protection against physical attacks ([arXiv 2507.02770](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02770v1)). Confidential computing protects data in use from the cloud operator. It does nothing against prompt injection.

The threat taxonomies are settled. **The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025** runs from LLM01 Prompt Injection to LLM10 Unbounded Consumption, which covers "denial of wallet" ([Invicti](https://www.invicti.com/blog/web-security/owasp-top-10-risks-llm-security-2025)). **The OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications (9 December 2025)** opens with ASI01 Agent Goal Hijack. Its example is EchoLeak, where a hidden instruction in an email made Microsoft 365 Copilot exfiltrate data with zero clicks. The list continues through tool misuse, identity and privilege abuse, the agentic supply chain, memory poisoning and rogue agents ([Giskard](https://www.giskard.ai/knowledge/owasp-top-10-for-agentic-application-2026); [Cycode](https://cycode.com/blog/owasp-top-10-agentic-applications/)). Two mental models predict when injection becomes an incident. Simon Willison's "lethal trifecta" says an agent with access to private data, exposure to untrusted content and a channel to communicate externally can be tricked into exfiltrating that data ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/jun/16/the-lethal-trifecta/)). Meta's "Agents Rule of Two" goes further and covers actions as well as theft. An agent should combine at most two of these properties: processing untrusted input, accessing sensitive systems, and changing state or communicating externally. An agent that needs all three needs human supervision ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Nov/2/new-prompt-injection-papers/)). Auto-rendered markdown images are the classic exfiltration channel ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/tags/markdown-exfiltration/)). The supply chain now includes models and MCP servers. "nullifAI" models on Hugging Face hid malicious pickle files inside 7z-compressed archives that evaded the Picklescan scanner ([The Hacker News](https://thehackernews.com/2025/02/malicious-ml-models-found-on-hugging.html)). The safetensors format has no executable code path. OpenSSF Model Signing v1.0 (4 April 2025) proves a model's integrity and provenance, but not that it is benign ([OpenSSF](https://openssf.org/blog/2025/04/04/launch-of-model-signing-v1-0-openssf-ai-ml-working-group-secures-the-machine-learning-supply-chain/)).

Defenses split into probabilistic filters and architectural controls. The filters are useful but bypassable. Bedrock Guardrails offers content filters including prompt-attack detection, PII masking and contextual grounding checks, and its `ApplyGuardrail` API also works with non-Bedrock models ([AWS](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/bedrock/latest/userguide/guardrails-components.html)). Its Automated Reasoning checks reached GA in August 2025 ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/about-aws/whats-new/2025/08/automated-reasoning-checks-amazon-bedrock-guardrails)). Azure Prompt Shields detect direct and indirect attacks, but indirect detection requires you to mark document boundaries with delimiters ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry-classic/foundry-models/concepts/content-filter)). Llama Guard 4 and NeMo Guardrails protect at runtime, while garak and PyRIT test ([TraeAI](https://learn.traeai.com/t/ai-engineering/phases/18-ethics-safety-alignment/16-red-team-tooling-garak-llamaguard-pyrit)). **The defenses that hold are architectural.** CaMeL is the clearest example. A privileged LLM plans only from the trusted user query. A quarantined LLM parses untrusted data with no tool access. An interpreter tracks data provenance before every tool call. On the AgentDojo benchmark it solved **77% of tasks with provable security, against 84% undefended**. For Claude 3.5 Sonnet, successful attacks fell from 44 to zero ([CaMeL, arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.18813v2); [SaTML 2026](https://www.computer.org/csdl/proceedings-article/satml/2026/785900a587/2jZks3ksGT6)). That seven-point utility cost is today's price of provable injection resistance. Expect production systems to apply CaMeL-style data-flow policies only on high-risk tool paths. Ranked by reliability, the defense stack runs from **architecture** to **least privilege**, then **containment**, **detection** and **assurance**. Architecture means separating plans from data, applying the Rule of Two and never auto-rendering external URLs. Least privilege means per-user on-behalf-of tokens and narrowly scoped tools. Containment means sandboxes, egress allowlists and budgets. Detection means the guardrail classifiers. Assurance means red teaming in CI mapped to MITRE ATLAS, which grew to about 120 techniques by v2026.09 with new autonomous-agent techniques, though secondary counts conflict ([CallSphere](https://callsphere.ai/blog/td30-rp-mitre-atlas-2026-update)).

Governance deadlines moved. **The EU's Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026) deferred high-risk obligations but left GPAI and transparency duties on schedule** ([Usercentrics](https://usercentrics.com/knowledge-hub/eu-ai-act-high-risk-delay-article-50-transparency-consent/); [CSA](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-eu-ai-act-high-risk-deadline-omnibus-20260/)). The Official Journal text was not retrieved for this report; the dates below come from law-firm and advisory summaries that agree with each other.

GPAI models are presumed to pose systemic risk above 10²⁵ FLOPs of training compute ([EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)). Whether the omnibus changed the AI literacy duty is unresolved. In the US, NIST's AI Risk Management Framework (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) and its Generative AI Profile (AI 600-1, July 2024) are the baseline ([NIST AI 600-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)). A Cyber AI Profile (IR 8596) appeared as a preliminary draft on 16 December 2025 ([NIST CSRC](https://csrc.nist.gov/News/2025/nist-releases-prelim-draft-cyber-ai-profile)). HIPAA has no certification. Compliance depends on what each business associate agreement (BAA) covers, feature by feature. AWS's BAA is self-service through AWS Artifact and covers Claude on Bedrock ([Aptible](https://www.aptible.com/hipaa-compliant-ai-tools/aws-bedrock-baa)). OpenAI offers an API BAA, but its web search is documented as not HIPAA-eligible ([CallSphere](https://callsphere.ai/blog/vw1f-hipaa-eligible-model-providers-2026)). Secondary sources conflict on what Anthropic's BAA covers ([Strac](https://www.strac.io/blog/is-claude-hipaa-compliant)). The cloud controls are how you evidence compliance. Invocation logs and audit trails support record-keeping duties. KMS and private networking support ISO/IEC 42001 and SOC 2 controls. An AI inventory listing each model, vendor, use case, risk tier and owner ties it together.

### Cost and FinOps: idle GPUs and broken caches cost more than any list price

FinOps (cloud financial operations) has pivoted to AI. **98% of State of FinOps 2026 respondents manage AI spend, up from 63% a year earlier** ([Flexera](https://www.flexera.com/blog/finops/finops-x-2026-recap)). FOCUS, the open billing-data specification, reached version 1.4 in June 2026. Version 1.5, targeted for December 2026, is slated to add native AI token tracking and a price-sheet dataset ([Finout](https://finout.io/blog/finops-x-2026-conference-recap-key-takeaways-and-product-launches)). The Linux Foundation also announced a sister Tokenomics Foundation ([TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/searchcio/news/366644142/linux-foundation-announces-tokenomicon-at-finops-x)). The FinOps Foundation's unit-economics framing treats cost per token as an efficiency metric. Cost per resolved case or per agent action is the outcome metric to report ([Finout](https://www.finout.io/blog/token-economics-and-tokenops-the-definitive-guide-to-finops-for-tokens)).

GPU utilization is the first lever, and it is usually mismeasured. **nvidia-smi's "GPU-Util" reports the fraction of time any kernel was running.** One tiny kernel on one streaming multiprocessor (SM) reads the same 100% as a kernel filling the whole chip ([Arthur Chiao](https://arthurchiao.art/blog/understanding-gpu-performance/)). Use DCGM's SM activity (`DCGM_FI_PROF_SM_ACTIVE`) and tensor-pipe activity instead. An SM activity of 0.8 or above is necessary but not sufficient for good use ([Netdata](https://www.netdata.cloud/guides/nvidia-gpu/nvidia-gpu-dcgm-vs-nvidia-smi-nvml/)). Real utilization is dismal. Cast AI's vendor telemetry finds **about 5% average GPU utilization across non-optimized Kubernetes clusters** ([VentureBeat via finanzen.net](https://www.finanzen.net/nachricht/aktien/5-gpu-utilization-the-401-billion-ai-infrastructure-problem-enterprises-can-t-keep-ignoring-15671084)). In a survey, 86% of enterprises running their own GPUs reported 50% utilization or less ([RuntimeWire](https://runtimewire.com/article/enterprise-gpu-utilization-ai-agents-venturebeat-survey)). Another found 44% still assign workloads to GPUs manually ([HackerNoon](https://hackernoon.com/nearly-half-of-enterprises-waste-millions-on-underutilized-gpu-capacity)). **Effective cost per useful GPU-hour is the list price divided by utilization.** At the $3.17 H100 index, 5% utilization means $63.40 per useful hour, 50% means $6.34 and 80% means $3.96. Raising utilization from 5% to 50% saves 10×, far more than any commitment discount. Three moves get you there. Serve FP8 models of 7B or smaller with modest concurrency on MIG slices or L4- and L40S-class cards rather than full H100s. Alert when an allocated GPU's SM activity stays below about 0.1, which catches idle notebooks and stalled jobs. Charge idle cost to the owning team through OpenCost- or Kubecost-style allocation, so teams have a reason to release GPUs.

The purchasing math is simple but often skipped. **A commitment at discount d beats on-demand only if you use more than (1 − d) of what you committed to.** A 40% discount therefore needs over 60% sustained use. A 64-GPU, 14-day H100 run uses 21,504 GPU-hours. It costs about $68k at the $3.17 index, about $128k at the October AWS Capacity Block rate and about $148k at $6.88 hyperscaler on-demand. Spot at 60% off the index costs about $27k, before restarts and lost progress. Reported spot interruption rates for H100s range from about 4% to 20% per hour ([Thunder Compute](https://www.thundercompute.com/blog/should-i-use-cloud-gpu-spot-instances)). Capacity Blocks buy guaranteed capacity, not a discount. They are charged up front, so unused hours are sunk cost ([AWS Capacity Blocks pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/es/ec2/capacityblocks/pricing/)).

For API spend, Anthropic's October 2026 price list and its published internal measurements give the most complete public evidence on which levers matter.

| Model | Input ($/MTok) | 5-minute cache write | Cache read | Output | Batch (input / output) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Claude Fable 5.1 | $10 | $12.50 | $0.25 | $50 | $5 / $25 |
| Claude Opus 5.5 | $4 | $5 | $0.20 | $20 | $2 / $10 |
| Claude Sonnet 5.5 | $2 | $2.50 | $0.10 | $10 | $1 / $5 |
| Claude Haiku 5.5 (prompts ≤100K tokens) | $0.10 | $0.125 | $0.01 | $0.50 | $0.05 / $0.25 |

Sources: [Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing); [Anthropic cost-optimization guide](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/optimizing-for-cost-and-intelligence.md).

Output costs 5× input, and cache multipliers stack with the batch discount. Anthropic's internal measurements quantify each lever ([Anthropic cost-optimization guide](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/optimizing-for-cost-and-intelligence.md)). Caching matters most. Across a day of real traffic, agent loops read a **median 84% of their input from cache**. Running without caching cost 3.3× on Opus 5.5 (SWE-bench Pro), 3.1× on Sonnet 5.5, 1.7× on Haiku 5.5 and **7.3×** for a Sonnet 5.5 bug-triage agent. A single broken cache turn on a 100K-token prefix costs $0.50 instead of a $0.02 read on Opus 5.5, a 25× penalty. Effort and model choice come next. On a SWE-bench Pro subset, Sonnet 5.5 at high effort solved 87.6% of tasks at **$0.11 per solved task**, against $0.22 for Opus 5.5 at medium effort (92.8%). Running everything at low effort and re-running only the failures at high effort reached about 97% at $0.17 per task, against 95.3% at $0.29 for all-high, roughly 40% cheaper. That trick needs a reliable failure signal, such as tests. A too-low `max_tokens` backfires. Opus 5.5 capped at 16,384 tokens solved 57.6% of tasks, against 75.6% at 64,000, for the same cost per solved task. Passing data files through code execution instead of pasting them into the prompt cut cost from $5.01 to $0.40 per run, while accuracy rose from 7/25 to 25/25. Model upgrades usually cut cost per solved task; Sonnet 5 to 5.5 cut it 87%. The exception was Fable 5.1, which cost 46% more per task than Fable 5. Claude 4.7 and later models also produce about 30% more tokens for the same text, so compare cost per task, not list price per token ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). Anthropic's recommended order is caching and trimming, then batch, then a prompt audit, then effort and model choice, with multi-model cascades last. Prompt caches are per-model, so a cascade forfeits cross-model cache hits. OpenAI and Gemini prices circulate mainly through aggregators that contradict each other, so budget from the providers' own pages.

Two worked examples show the arithmetic. Take a support chatbot on Sonnet 5.5 with a 3,000-token cacheable system prompt and tools block, 1,000 uncached tokens and 400 output tokens per request. It costs $0.012 per request uncached and $0.0063 on a cache hit, about 47% less. At an 80% hit rate, a million requests cost about $7,740. Now consider a cascade that sends everything to Haiku 5.5 first and escalates 30% to Sonnet 5.5. It costs about $4,200 per million requests, against $12,000 for all-Sonnet (−65%). That saving holds only if the escalation classifier is reliable and the quality on the 70% that stay on Haiku is acceptable.

The self-hosting formula is cost per token = GPU price per hour ÷ (tokens per second × 3,600 × utilization). One vendor estimates about 400 tokens/s for a 70B FP8 model on one H100, which is conservative for well-batched serving ([Spheron](https://www.spheron.network/blog/migrate-from-openai-api-to-self-hosted-llms-cost-2026/)). At the $3.17 index that is **$2.20 per million tokens at full utilization and $5.50 at 40%**. One H100 running 24/7 costs about $76 a day. Against Opus 5.5's blended $7.20 per million tokens (80% input, 20% output), it breaks even at about 10.6M tokens a day, and against Sonnet 5.5 at about 21.1M. It never breaks even against Haiku 5.5: matching Haiku's price would take about 423M tokens a day, far beyond one GPU's roughly 35M-token daily capacity. Published estimates agree on the shape. Two H100s running gpt-oss-120b cost about 2× a hosted provider's price for the same model ([General Compute](https://www.generalcompute.com/blog/vllm-vs-managed-inference-apis-when-to-self-host-vs-pay-as-you-go?agent=true)), and production utilization of 30–60% makes per-token cost 2–3× the peak estimate ([Codewave](https://codewave.com/feeds/blog/self-hosted-llm-cost)). Self-hosting wins in four cases: steady high-volume traffic on small or fine-tuned models, strict data residency, latency targets APIs cannot meet, and owned GPUs with idle headroom.

Cost attribution needs metadata captured at request time, because LLM API calls carry no resource tags. Stamp every call at the gateway with team, feature, tenant, environment, model and trace ID, plus the four token counts and a cost computed from a versioned price table ([Gravitee](https://gravitee.io/corpus/gen-1210/ocnosispa/ocnosispa-llm-gateway-finops-usage-metering-chargeback-and-cost-allocation.html)). Anthropic's Usage and Cost Admin API groups usage by model, key and workspace, but excludes traffic through Bedrock, Vertex and Foundry ([Anthropic Usage and Cost API](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/usage-cost-api.md)). Start with showback, which only reports costs, and move to chargeback, which bills teams, once the allocation rules are stable. The most valuable anomaly alert is a sudden drop in the ratio of cache reads to total input. A timestamp added to a system prompt can multiply input cost overnight. Network charges hide in training too. **AWS bills cross-AZ traffic at $0.01/GB in each direction**, about $0.02 effective ([Last Week in AWS](https://www.lastweekinaws.com/blog/aws-cross-az-data-transfer-costs-more-than-aws-says)). Hourly 70B full-state checkpoints written across zones would cost about $538 a day. Never stripe a training cluster across availability zones, and expire old checkpoints with lifecycle rules. On sustainability, carbon-aware scheduling suits long training runs, but measured gains are modest. One 2026 study of three AI data-center parks reported about 4.5% lower carbon and 1.7% lower cost ([arXiv 2609.11649](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.11649); [University of Alberta](https://sites.ualberta.ca/~oardakan/files/PESGM26.pdf)). Region choice is the bigger lever. A 64-H100, two-week run uses about 18 MWh at a PUE of 1.2. Using illustrative grid intensities, that is roughly 0.9 tonnes of CO₂e on a hydro- or nuclear-heavy grid and roughly 9 tonnes on a coal-heavy one.

## A six-stage learning path fits inside about $250

Employers set the curriculum. An analysis of 43,480 US AI-engineering postings through July 2026 found **Python in 62%, cloud platforms in 55% and foundation-model work in 51%**. Postings ran at about 1,550 new roles a week and centered on people 3–7 years into their careers ([Axial Search](https://axialsearch.com/insights/ai-ml-engineering-jobs/)). ML-platform and LLMOps postings add Terraform, Kubernetes with multi-GPU orchestration, and serving stacks such as vLLM, SGLang, Triton and Ray Serve, "with attention to GPU utilization, latency, and throughput" ([Turing/Greenhouse posting](https://job-boards.greenhouse.io/turingportal/jobs/5537879004); [Dice posting](https://www.dice.com/job-detail/92bc7960-ba8d-4a26-a144-0413768e1899)). Market-size headlines conflict wildly and are best ignored. The profile that gets rewarded is Python plus one hyperscaler in depth, and foundation-model apps with RAG, agents and evals. Add Kubernetes and Terraform for GPU workloads, a serving stack, and observability.

Learn the fundamentals in the order the job punishes mistakes: IAM, then VPC networking and private endpoints, then object storage, then GPU instance families, quotas and spot, then billing controls. The providers' starter credits fund most of this. AWS accounts opened after 15 July 2025 get $100 in credit and can earn up to $100 more. Google Cloud gives $300 for 90 days and Azure $200 for 30 days ([freetier.co](https://freetier.co/articles/cloud-provider-free-tiers-2026)). Kaggle offers about 30 free GPU-hours a week ([gpuperhour](https://gpuperhour.com/blog/free-cloud-gpus-and-credits)). The billing traps are part of the lesson. An EKS cluster on an extended-support Kubernetes version costs about $0.60 per cluster-hour instead of $0.10 ([TechTarget](https://www.techtarget.com/it-infrastructure/tip/Break-down-the-Amazon-EKS-pricing-model)). GKE's free-tier credit does not cover regional Standard clusters ([Finout](https://www.finout.io/blog/gke-pricing-tiers)). A spot job without checkpoints that gets evicted can cost more than running it on demand ([GPUnex](https://www.gpunex.com/blog/cloud-gpu-pricing-comparison-2026)). ## Conclusion

The durable asset for an AI engineer is not any product. Products churned within twelve months: Vertex AI became the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform, and the Assistants API shut down. OpenAI stopped accepting new fine-tuning customers. TGI, torchtune and CML went dormant. Training Operator v1 gave way to TrainJob, and four major cloud AI certifications retired. What survived intact is reasoning in bytes and queues: bytes per parameter, KV bytes per token, bytes per step across which link and bytes per dollar, plus goodput, utilization and cache-hit rate on top. An engineer who can derive a 21 ms decode floor, a 16-byte ledger, a three-hour MTBF and a cache break-even on paper can evaluate any new GPU, serving engine or managed API in minutes. Most vendor benchmarks in this space could not be independently verified, and that same reasoning also makes them far less persuasive.

The year also overturned two economic assumptions. GPU-hour prices stopped falling predictably and rose. Meanwhile, token prices for a given model tier fell sharply between generations. Anthropic's small-model tier dropped tenfold, from Haiku 4.5 at $1/$5 to Haiku 5.5 at $0.10/$0.50 per million input/output tokens ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)). The self-hosting break-even therefore keeps moving toward APIs for everything except steady, high-volume or data-sovereign workloads. The engineer's leverage shifts from procuring GPUs to raising utilization, cache-hit rate and cost per successful task. At the same time, the stack's control points are consolidating under a few owners. NVIDIA now holds SchedMD, KAI and Dynamo plus its Groq license, CoreWeave owns Weights & Biases, OpenAI is acquiring Promptfoo and ClickHouse acquired Langfuse. That makes open interfaces the practical hedge against lock-in: OpenTelemetry GenAI, MCP, the Gateway API Inference Extension and Iceberg REST catalogs. The security lesson runs in parallel. The incidents that mattered were system-level failures that no classifier caught, and the defenses that hold are architectural, such as the Rule of Two and CaMeL. AI security is now a design skill you practice, not a filter you buy.
