Compute & Infrastructure

Oct 2026

  • Fit first. Per-GPU memory now comes in 3 tiers → 80 GB · 141–192 GB · 288 GB. HBM bandwidth roughly doubles per generation (3.4 → 4.8 → 8 → ~22 TB/s), and bandwidth sets decode speed.
  • The scale-up domain matters as much as the chip: 8-GPU HGX node vs 72-GPU NVL72 rack vs 144-chip Trn3 UltraServer vs 9,216-chip TPU pod. MoE serving wants the big domains.
  • Status, Oct 2026: H100/H200/B200 are the workhorses. B300/GB300 is broadly rentable. Vera Rubin is limited availability only (CoreWeave, 30 Sep 2026). MI355X ships; MI455X is announced. TPU7x Ironwood and Trainium3 are GA.
  • Buying ladder: on-demand → 1–3 yr commits (up to ~55% off) → prepaid windows (AWS Capacity Blocks, DWS Calendar) → queued (DWS Flex-start, ~50% off TPU) → spot (up to 91% off; 2 min notice on AWS, 30 s on Azure).
  • Autoscale nodes with Cluster Autoscaler/Karpenter and pods with KEDA. Scale-to-zero only works if a cold start of tens of seconds to minutes is OK → cache weights + compile artifacts, snapshot, or keep 1 warm.
  • Neoclouds often list at ~½–⅔ of hyperscaler $/GPU-hr and get new silicon first. Hyperscalers win on data gravity, IAM/VPC, managed services and their own chips (TPU, Trainium).

The accelerator map (Oct 2026)

Four numbers decide almost everything. ← learn what each one buys you

  • HBM capacity → what fits on one chip (weights + KV cache).
  • HBM bandwidth → decode tokens/s, because decode is memory-bound.
  • Dense FP8/FP4 FLOPs → prefill, batch jobs and training speed.
  • Scale-up domain (NVLink / ICI / NeuronLink) → how far tensor/expert parallelism can go before traffic falls onto the slower network.

Memory tiers

80 → ~141/192 → 288. 80 = H100. ~141–192 = H200, B200, GB200, MI300X. 288 = B300, GB300, MI355X, Rubin.

Bandwidth ladder: 3.4 → 4.8 → 8 → ~22 TB/s (H100 → H200 → B200/B300 → Rubin).

NVIDIA: Hopper → Blackwell → Blackwell Ultra → Rubin

Hopper (H100, H200)

  • Same compute, more memory: H200 = H100 FLOPs + 141 GB at 4.8 TB/s → better for decode-heavy, long-context serving.
  • Node = 8 GPUs + 4 NVSwitches. NVLink 4 = 18 links × 25 GB/s = 450 GB/s per direction.
  • Scale-out: 8 × 400 Gbps ConnectX-7 NICs ≈ 400 GB/s per node (~50 GB/s per GPU) into fat-tree InfiniBand. SuperPOD "Scalable Unit" = 32 nodes = 256 GPUs.
  • ⇒ leaving the node is ~9× slower than NVLink (450 vs ~50 GB/s per GPU). That's why the NVLink domain matters.

Blackwell (B200, GB200)

  • ~2.3× H100 compute, adds FP4. NVLink 5 = 18 × 50 GB/s = 900 GB/s per direction (1.8 TB/s bidirectional).
  • HGX B200 = 8-GPU domain. GB200 NVL72 = 72 GPUs + Grace CPUs on one NVLink fabric.
  • "Node" ≠ "host" now: an Azure GB200 VM = 4 GPUs on one Grace host, inside an NVLink domain of up to 72.

Blackwell Ultra (B300, GB300 NVL72) GA

  • Per GPU: 288 GB at ~8 TB/s, ~15 PF dense FP4 (20 sparse), ~1,400 W, dual-die. Rack: 72 B300 + 36 Grace, ~20 TB HBM, ~1.1 EF FP4 dense.
  • Azure Standard_ND128isr_GB300_v6: 4 × 288 GB, 128 vCPU, 864 GiB RAM, 4 × 800 Gbps InfiniBand, scale-out "up to 100,000 GPUs".

Vera Rubin LIMITED → ramping

  • Per GPU (CES/GTC 2026 coverage, ≈): 288 GB HBM4, ~22 TB/s (raised from a 13 TB/s target), ~50 PF NVFP4 inference / ~35 training (≈5× / 3.5× B200), TSMC N3, 2 dies. NVLink 6 = 3.6 TB/s per GPU. Rack ≈ 3.6 EF NVFP4 inference.
  • Naming trap: the same rack was first marketed as "NVL144" (counting dies). Now "Vera Rubin NVL72".
  • NVIDIA, Jul 2026: shipping to clouds (AWS, Google Cloud, Azure, OCI named early). CoreWeave: NVL72 limited availability, 30 Sep 2026. First customer Cognition claims 4.8× token throughput vs GB200 NVL72.
  • No hyperscaler Rubin GA confirmed by mid-Oct 2026; one vendor blog expects most teams to wait until 2027.

Why bandwidth matters so much for chat: every decode step must stream all the weights (plus each sequence's KV cache) out of HBM.

t_step ≥ (B × KV_seq + W) ÷ BW_HBMB = batch, KV_seq = KV bytes per sequence, W = weight bytes, BW_HBM = total HBM bandwidth. A floor, not a benchmark.
  • 70B @ FP8 (W ≈ 70 GB), batch 1: H200 → 70 GB ÷ 4.8 TB/s ≈ 15 ms/token (≤ ~70 tok/s). B300 ≈ 9 ms. Rubin ≈ 3 ms. ← arithmetic upper bound
  • Rubin's ~22 TB/s helps decode most; its 50 PF FP4 helps prefill and training most.

Rule: large MoE or >8-way tensor/expert parallelism → NVL72-class systems, so all-to-all stays on NVLink.

Choosing a GPU: fit → bandwidth → FLOPs → interconnect

  1. Does it fit? Weights + KV cache + ~20–40% headroom (activations, CUDA graphs, fragmentation).
  2. Decode-heavy (chat, long context) → maximize HBM bandwidth + capacity per $: H200, B300, MI355X, Ironwood.
  3. Prefill-heavy, batch, training → maximize dense FP8/FP4 FLOPs per $ + interconnect.
  4. MoE or >8-way TP/EP → NVL72, Trn3 Gen2 or TPU ICI domains, not 8-GPU nodes over InfiniBand.
  5. Small models, embeddings → L4, RTX PRO 6000, Inferentia2, v5e, often on serverless.
serving: W ≈ P × bP = params, b = bytes/param: BF16 = 2 · FP8/INT8 = 1 · FP4/INT4 = 0.5
KV = 2 × b × H × K × L × T2 = K and V · H = head dim · K = KV heads · L = layers · T = tokens (all sequences). LLaMA-13B, one 8,192-token sequence, BF16 → 6.7 GB; 4 such sequences > the 26 GB of weights.

KV shrinks >10× with GQA/MQA, KV quantization, paging (PagedAttention), cross-layer sharing → deep dive in topic 5.

Training memory + FLOPs (brief → topic 4 goes deep)

train ≥ 10 B/param · classic ≈ 16 B/param10 = BF16 weights (2) + FP32 Adam moments (8). 16 = BF16 weights + BF16 grads + FP32 master, momentum, variance (2 + 2 + 12, ZeRO paper). Activations come on top.
  • Pure data parallel: biggest model ≈ HBM ÷ 10 → ~9B on a 96 GB v5p (lower bound).
  • Activations often dominate: 13B → ~130 GB params + Adam, but ~42 TB checkpointed activations at a 16M-token batch.
  • ZeRO-1/2/3 shard optimizer / grads / weights; ZeRO-3 (FSDP) costs no extra comms volume vs plain DP.
train FLOPs ≈ 6 × N × D · infer ≈ 2 × N per tokenN = params, D = training tokens. Block remat (recompute) → ~8 × N × D.

Critical batch: decode stays memory-bound until batch > B_crit ≈ FLOPs ÷ HBM BW (~240 for TPU v5e BF16; H100 ≈ 990 ÷ 3.4 ≈ 290). Int8/fp8 weights halve B_crit; doing the math in int8/fp8 too doubles it back. ⇒ Batching turns bandwidth into throughput.

  • "Fits" ≠ "serves." 70B BF16 on one H200 fits the weights and leaves ~0 GB for KV → OOM under real load.
  • Datasheet ≠ usable HBM: B200 192 GB vs ~180 in some listings; one GB300 datasheet says 279, not 288.
  • Comparing sparse vs dense or FP4 vs FP8 peaks (B300 "20 PF" is sparse). Compare dense, same precision.
  • Picking by FLOPs for a chat app: decode is bandwidth-bound.
Bytes/param chant

"Serve 2-1-½, train 16."

BF16 = 2, FP8 = 1, FP4 = ½ for inference. Full Adam training ≈ 16 (floor 10). So a 7B full fine-tune ≈ 112 GB: already bigger than an H100.

Spot interruption notice

  • AWS: 2 minutes, via EventBridge/CloudWatch events + instance metadata. Exception: hibernation starts immediately, with no 2-min warning.
  • Azure: 30 seconds, best-effort, via Scheduled Events. Deallocate (default) or Delete. Max price -1 = no price-based eviction. Portal shows 7-day eviction rates. No SLA.
  • GCP: reportedly ~30 s too (unverified) → plan like Azure.
  • Karpenter reads an SQS interruption queue (EventBridge), drains and starts a replacement in parallel; node startup generally fits in the 2 min.

Checkpointing for preemptible capacity

checkpoint ≈ P × ~16 Bfull state (weights + optimizer) → 70B ≈ 1.1 TB. Weights-only BF16 = P × 2 → 140 GB.
  • 30 s can't write a TB-scale checkpoint → checkpoint on a schedule, async + sharded (PyTorch DCP, Orbax) to fast storage.
  • AWS's 2 min allows a final "emergency" sharded save for small/mid models, not 70B full state.
  • Interval: you lose ~½ an interval per eviction. Young's rule: interval ≈ √(2 × save time × mean time between evictions) → 2 min save, eviction every ~10 h ⇒ ≈ 49 min.

Autoscaling GPU workloads

Two layers. Node level: Cluster Autoscaler (CA) or Karpenter adds/removes GPU machines. Pod level: HPA, or KEDA for event/queue-driven scaling, including to and from zero.

Cluster Autoscaler, GPU specifics

  • --scale-down-gpu-utilization-threshold, default 0.5. On GPU nodes only GPU requests count; CPU and memory are ignored.
  • ⇒ 8-GPU node with 3 GPUs requested = 37.5% → scale-down candidate. Many small pods on big nodes can thrash → use smaller GPU shapes, or MIG/time-slicing for small models.
  • --gpu-total caps the count per GPU type ("currently only works on GKE").
  • Nodes whose GPU driver hasn't come up show as resourceUnready (CA ≥ 1.24) → CA treats them as not-yet-ready instead of adding more nodes.

Karpenter

  • Right-sized nodes straight from pending pods. Separate NodePools per GPU type and for spot vs on-demand.
  • Rebalance Recommendations only raise events; draining on them needs the AWS Node Termination Handler (more churn).

Cold starts: the core problem

Cold start = request arrives with no running worker → worker warm (container start, model load to GPU, runtime init, compile). LLMs: tens of seconds to a few minutes.

  • Modal: GPU memory snapshots (experimental): load inside @modal.enter(snap=True), later starts restore it. Deployed Functions only.

Docker for ML: who owns which piece of the GPU stack

Containers share the host kernel → the GPU kernel driver can't live in the image. How the work splits: ← learn this cold

  • Kernel driver + user-mode driver (libcuda.so) → on the host, or in the GPU Operator's driver container. Never baked into the app image.
  • CUDA Toolkit (nvcc, headers) → only needed in a build stage (the -devel tag).
  • CUDA runtime libs (cudart, cuBLAS, cuDNN, NCCL) → ship in the image: -runtime / -cudnn-runtime tags, or the nvidia-* pip wheels that PyTorch pulls in.
  • NVIDIA Container Toolkit (on the host) → at container start, injects the device nodes (/dev/nvidia*) and the host's driver libs.

The compatibility rule

host driver ≥ min driver for the image's CUDA majoroften-quoted minimums: R525+ for CUDA 12.x, R580+ for CUDA 13.x (check NVIDIA's CUDA Compatibility guide). Within one major → minor-version compat. cuda-compat package = forward compat (newer CUDA on an older datacenter driver).
  • Field drivers: GPU Operator 25.10 → 580.95.05; 26.3 → 580.126.20; 26.7.0 → 595.91.07; 26.7.1 adds 615.71.09 ⇒ 580 = CUDA 13-era baseline (my inference).

NVIDIA Container Toolkit + CDI

CDI (Container Device Interface) = a spec file (e.g. /etc/cdi/nvidia.yaml) listing each device's nodes, mounts and hooks. Runtimes read it directly → no "nvidia" runtime wrapper needed.

  • GPU Operator ≥ 25.10: CDI on by default (cdi.enabled=true; cdi.default deprecated/ignored).
  • GPU Operator 26.3: NRI plugin mode (containerd 1.7.30+, 2.1.x, 2.2.x; not CRI-O) → no nvidia RuntimeClass, containerd config left alone. 26.7 → minimum containerd 2.0.
  • Toolkit: v1.19 (Mar 2026) rejects unknown OCI spec fields → v1.20.0 (Aug) exact driver-version matching in the compat hook → v1.20.1 (19 Sep 2026) MIG injection in CDI mode + per-GPU CUDA memory limits (R615+ driver; Operator 26.7.1 env NVIDIA_GPU_MEMORY_REQUEST / _LIMIT).

Kubernetes GPU scheduling: device plugin, GPU Operator, sharing

Device plugin (the classic path)

  • A DaemonSet advertises nvidia.com/gpu as an extended resource. Pods ask in limits: whole integers, no overcommit, requests = limits.
  • Prereqs: driver + container toolkit + nvidia runtime as default (or RuntimeClass nvidia).
  • Device-list strategies: envvar (default, NVIDIA_VISIBLE_DEVICES), volume-mounts, cdi-annotations, cdi-cri. DEVICE_ID_STRATEGY = uuid (default) or index.
  • GPU Feature Discovery (GFD) labels nodes: nvidia.com/gpu.product, nvidia.com/gpu.memory → use them in nodeSelector / affinity.
  • Taint GPU pools (e.g. nvidia.com/gpu=present:NoSchedule) so CPU-only pods don't land on expensive nodes. GPU pods carry the toleration. EKS/GKE/AKS each have their own default taint conventions.

Why gang scheduling at all?

Distributed training needs all N workers up or nobody makes progress. The default scheduler places pods one at a time → job A grabs 3 of its 4 nodes, job B grabs the 4th, and both sit idle on $$$ GPUs = partial-placement deadlock. Gang = place all or none.

  • 1.37 native API: gang policy has minCount (now mutable → elastic resize); the basic policy schedules pods one by one; disruptionMode = all / single (renamed); only the top-level PodGroup is queued.
  • To use it: enable GenericWorkload on apiserver, controller-manager and scheduler. JobSet integration → 1.38. Kueue has no WAS integration yet (long-term: WAS becomes Kueue's engine).
  • Press claims of "gang on by default in 1.37" → wrong per the official post.

Batch queueing & gang tools: Kueue, JobSet, LWS, Volcano, KAI

Kueue = job-level admission + quota

  • Decides when a job starts (unsuspends it) and when its pods stop. The kube-scheduler (or KAI/Volcano) still decides where the pods go.
  • Concepts: ResourceFlavor (a node type, e.g. "h100" = labels + tolerations), ClusterQueue (quota per flavor), LocalQueue (per-namespace entry point), Workload (Kueue's view of a job), cohorts (queues lend/borrow idle quota), fair sharing, preemption, StrictFIFO / BestEffortFIFO.

The rest of the toolbox

  • LeaderWorkerSet (LWS): a pod group is the unit of replication → multi-host inference. Leader + worker templates, opt-in all-or-nothing group restart, group rolling updates, HPA. DisaggregatedSet (prefill/decode) builds on it with llm-d.
  • Volcano: CNCF incubating batch scheduler (from kube-batch); network-topology-aware "HyperNode" scheduling for LLM training.
  • KAI Scheduler (NVIDIA, from Run:ai; CNCF sandbox): gang, hierarchical queues, DRF + time-based fair share, GPU sharing, TAS, consolidation, DRA support.

Node failure in big training runs

  1. Assume failure. At scale, a dead GPU or node is routine, not rare.
  2. Checkpoint often to a parallel FS / object storage (async, sharded).
  3. Gang restart: JobSet failurePolicy, TrainJob, Kueue requeue, LWS group restart, Slurm --requeue, 1.37 disruptionMode: all.
  4. Hot spares / an over-provisioned pool so a replacement doesn't wait on capacity. Kueue v0.20.1 fixed TAS failed-node replacement.
Failure loop

Detect → Taint → Evict → Replace → Resume. DCGM/XID spots it, a taint fences it, the gang restarts on a spare, training resumes from the last checkpoint.

Run dcgmi diag + an NCCL all-reduce test as a pre-flight before a node joins the training pool. A slow-but-"Ready" node drags every rank down to its speed.

Containers share the host kernel → driver + libcuda.so live on the host. The image carries the CUDA runtime libs; the toolkit injects devices + driver libs at start. Host driver must meet the minimum for the image's CUDA major.

Data & Storage

Oct 2026

  • Regional object storage (S3, GCS, Azure Blob) = durable source of truth. Zonal tiers (S3 Express One Zone, GCS Rapid, Anywhere Cache) + Lustre = fast, re-creatable copies next to the GPUs.
  • Training input: 100 MB–1 GB sequential shards (WebDataset, Mosaic MDS), streamed. Never random-read millions of small files.
  • Lakehouse (Delta / Iceberg v3 + a catalog) = system of record. Vector indexes + online feature stores are derived → rebuildable.
  • ANN = recall vs latency vs memory. HNSW in RAM for hot data; IVF+PQ / DiskANN / S3 Vectors for big + cheap. BM25 + vector → RRF → rerank = standard RAG retrieval.
  • Bytes per vector = d × bytes/dim. int8 = 4× smaller, binary = 32× (rescore to recover), Matryoshka truncation stacks on top.
  • Point-in-time joins, pinned snapshots, streaming upserts, PII scrubbed before embedding.

Object storage = the source of truth

Corpora, shards, checkpoints, weights, evals, logs → all in buckets. Cheap per GB, very durable, scales by parallelism (prefixes × connections), not one fat stream.

S3 facts worth knowing

  • Strong read-after-write consistency (incl. LIST), automatic + free since Dec 2020.
  • ≥ 3,500 PUT/COPY/POST/DELETE + 5,500 GET/HEAD per second per partitioned prefix. Unlimited prefixes → spread your keys.
  • Load spike → auto-partitioning takes ~30–60 min; meanwhile HTTP 503 "Slow Down" → retry with exponential backoff.
  • Conditional writes: If-None-Match: * (put-if-absent, Aug 2024) + If-Match on ETag (compare-and-swap, Nov 2024) → lock-free table-format commits, "object store as database".
GET/s ceiling ≈ 5,500 × (# hot prefixes)keys hashed over 16 prefixes → ~88k GET/s once S3 has partitioned them

The zonal "fast object" tier

  • S3 Express One Zone (Nov 2023): "directory buckets" in one AZ, single-digit-ms latency ("up to 10× faster" than Standard [vendor]), up to 2M GET / 200k PUT TPS per bucket. Apr 10, 2025 cuts: storage −31%, PUT −55%, GET −85%, per-GB charges −60%.
  • GCS Rapid Bucket (zonal, Next '25): sub-ms, up to 15 TB/s aggregate, 20M QPS; appendable objects → checkpoints + logs.
  • GCS Anywhere Cache: zonal SSD read cache in front of existing buckets, no API change; up to 2.5 TB/s, 70% lower latency [vendor].
  • Cloud Storage FUSE mounts buckets as files (one anecdote: Lustre → GCS FUSE = 50% lower cost, 14% shorter training).
  • Azure Blob: Hot / Cool / Cold / Archive + Premium block blobs; ADLS Gen2 adds a hierarchical namespace.

Feeding the GPUs: shards + streaming

Object stores love big sequential reads: sequential ~100 MB reads ≈ 80% of bandwidth; random 1 KB reads ≈ 1%. ⇒ pack samples into shards.

  • Shard = 100 MB–1 GB. Each loader worker holds ~1 shard + a shuffle buffer.
  • WebDataset: a convention, not a DB. Plain .tar shards; one sample = sibling files sharing a basename (000123.jpg, .txt, .json), read sequentially. Simple, good for multimodal.
  • Mosaic StreamingDataset: MDS shards (also CSV/TSV/JSONL) from any cloud or S3-compatible store (R2, CoreWeave, B2). Elastic determinism (same sample order for any GPU count), mid-epoch resume, local cache with eviction → go-to for resumable LLM pretraining on preemptible capacity.
  • AWS: Mountpoint for S3 (FUSE, GA 2023), S3 Connector for PyTorch (datasets + checkpoint save/load straight to S3).

Vector search: how ANN indexes work

Exact k-NN = O(N·d) per query → too slow at 10⁸+. ANN trades a little recall for big latency + memory wins. Measure recall@k = share of the true top-k returned. Metrics: L2, dot, cosine (Hamming for binary); use what the embedding model was trained with.

‖a − b‖² = 2 − 2·a·b (if ‖a‖ = ‖b‖ = 1)normalize once → cosine = dot, L2 gives the same ranking

HNSW: layered graph, the default

  • Malkov & Yashunin (2016/18). Top layers sparse (long jumps); layer 0 holds every vector.
  • Search: enter at top → greedy hop to the neighbor nearest q → stuck? drop a layer → beam of efSearch on layer 0 → top-k.
  • Knobs: M links per node, efConstruction build beam, efSearch query beam (↑ recall, ↑ latency). RAM-resident; deletes degrade the graph → rebuild.

Mnemonic

M = how many friends a node keeps. ef = how hard you look (while building / while querying). More friends → more RAM; look harder → slower.

IVF + PQ: cluster, then compress

  • IVF: k-means into nlist cells; probe the nprobe nearest cells.
  • PQ (Jégou et al., 2011): split vector into m sub-vectors, each → 1 of 256 centroids (1 byte); distances via lookup tables (ADC).

  • ScaNN (ICML 2020): anisotropic quantization, penalizing the error that hurts inner-product ranking. Behind BigQuery TreeAH, Vertex Vector Search, AlloyDB.

Offline store

Warehouse / lakehouse, full history → training sets via point-in-time "as-of" joins.

PII & residency: the basics

(Security in depth → Topic 8.) Core idea: embeddings + chunk text are personal data if the source was.

  1. Classify at ingestion: Amazon Macie, Google Sensitive Data Protection (masking, tokenization, format-preserving encryption), Microsoft Purview; tag in the catalog.
  2. Redact / tokenize before chunking, embedding, training.
  3. Propagate tags through lineage → indexes + feature tables inherit them.
  4. Enforce row filters / column masks (Unity Catalog, Snowflake) + per-user ACL filters at retrieval, or RAG leaks documents.

Erasure must hit every copy: source tables (+ VACUUM / expire snapshots), vector indexes (by doc_id), feature stores, backups, fine-tuned weights (practically impossible) ⇒ don't train on raw PII. Long time-travel retention fights erasure → choose it on purpose.

  • Derived data breaks residency too: embeddings, caches, logs, prompts sent to a model API in another region.

Training at Scale

Oct 2026

  • Memory first: mixed-precision Adam ≈ 16 bytes/param before activations → 7B full FT = 112 GB, 70B = 1.12 TB ⇒ shard (ZeRO-3 / FSDP2) + recompute activations.
  • Compute ≈ 6·N·D FLOPs. Good dense runs hit ~35–45% BF16 MFU on H100-class GPUs → GPU-hours = 6ND ÷ (peak × MFU).
  • Place parallelism by bandwidth: TP / SP / EP inside the NVLink domain (8 GPUs per node, 72 per NVL72 rack); PP and DP/FSDP across the ~400 Gb/s-per-GPU network (EFA, RoCE, InfiniBand).
  • Precision: BF16 is the default; FP8 is production-proven (DeepSeek-V3); NVFP4 matched FP8 on a 12B model over 10T tokens.
  • Failures are routine: Llama 3 had 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days (~1 every 3 h) → checkpoint every √(2·MTBF·C), save async, auto-resume.
  • Fine-tuning: LoRA/QLoRA for most jobs; SFT → DPO → RL (GRPO). OpenAI's self-serve fine-tuning is winding down (jobs end Jan 6, 2027) while Bedrock adds reinforcement fine-tuning.

Memory math: where the gigabytes go

Training memory = weights + gradients + optimizer states + activations (+ temp buffers, fragmentation). First three scale with params N. Activations scale with tokens in flight (seq × micro-batch) × hidden × layers.

The 16 bytes/param ledger (mixed-precision Adam)

  • BF16/FP16 weights → 2 B
  • BF16/FP16 gradients → 2 B
  • optimizer state: FP32 master weights 4 + Adam momentum m 4 + variance v 4 → 12 B ← ZeRO calls this K = 12
training state ≈ (2 + 2 + 12) × N = 16·N bytesZeRO paper (2020) writes it 16Ψ. Variants: 18 B (BF16 weights + FP32 grads + 12, no BF16 grad copy) and 20 B (ZeRO-Infinity counts both grad copies). Same ledger, different bookkeeping.
  • 7B → 7e9 × 16 = 112 GB → full fine-tune does not fit one 80 GB H100 without sharding or offload.
  • 70B → 1.12 TB → ZeRO-3 over 8 GPUs = 140 GB/GPU (still no); over 64 GPUs = 17.5 GB/GPU.
  • 405B → 6.48 TB of weights + grads + optimizer state.
  • Sharding changes each GPU's share, not the total.
Remember: 16 = 2 + 2 + 12

Two for the weights, two for the grads, twelve for Adam's baggage (FP32 copy + m + v). GB of training state ≈ 16 × billions of params.

Activations

act per layer ≈ s · b · h · (34 + 5·a·s / h) bytes16-bit activations, no recompute (Korthikanti et al. 2022). s = seq len, b = micro-batch, h = hidden size, a = attention heads
  • The 5·a·s/h term = attention scores + softmax → grows with s² → this is what kills long context.
  • Worked (70B-class layer: h = 8192, a = 64, s = 8192, b = 1): 23.8 GB/layer naive → 2.3 GB with selective recompute → 0.29 GB with TP = 8 + sequence parallel. × 80 layers ≈ 1.9 TB naive vs ≈ 23 GB.
  • FlashAttention never materializes the s × s score matrix → removes the 5as/h term in practice.

Activation checkpointing (recomputation)

  • Full: keep only each layer's input (s·b·h·2 B), re-run the forward during backward → ≈ +33% compute (≈ 8N FLOPs/token instead of 6N).
  • Selective: recompute only the attention part (memory-heavy, cheap to redo) → keeps ≈ 34·s·b·h. Paper: ~5× less activation memory; 530B on 2,240 A100s at ~54% MFU [2022].
  • HF Ultra-Scale Playbook (Feb 2025, 4,000+ runs on 1–512 GPUs): recompute is the main activation lever.

Compute math: 6ND, MFU and GPU-hours

training FLOPs ≈ 6 · N · DN = params (active params for MoE), D = training tokens. Forward ≈ 2 FLOPs per param per token, backward ≈ 4

  • HFU (hardware FLOPs utilization) also counts recompute → full checkpointing raises HFU (≈ 8N/token) but not MFU. ← quote MFU, not HFU
  • PaLM's MFU adds attention FLOPs: ≈ 6N + 12·L·h·s per token (L = layers).

6ND = "2 forward, 4 back"

Forward = 1 multiply + 1 add per param per token. Backward ≈ 2× forward (grads w.r.t. inputs and weights). Full recompute adds one more forward → 8.

Pipeline parallel and the bubble

bubble = (p − 1) / (m + p − 1) ≈ (p − 1) / m when m ≫ pp = pipeline stages, m = micro-batches per step. Same for GPipe and 1F1B
  • GPipe (2018): all forwards, then all backwards → stores activations for all m micro-batches.
  • 1F1B: same bubble, but alternates 1 fwd / 1 bwd after warm-up → only ~p micro-batches of activations in flight.
  • Interleaved 1F1B: v model chunks per GPU → bubble ÷ v (≈ (p − 1)/(v·m)); costs v× more point-to-point traffic + activation memory.
  • Zero Bubble: split backward into B (input-grad, on the critical path) and W (weight-grad, can wait) → slot W into idle gaps. ZB1P ≈ 1/3 of 1F1B's bubble at 1F1B memory; ZB2P ≈ zero bubble at ~2× activation memory.
  • DualPipe (DeepSeek-V3): bidirectional schedule → fewer bubbles and hides most communication (incl. MoE all-to-all) behind compute.
  • Worked: p = 8, m = 32 → 7/39 ≈ 18% idle; interleaved v = 4 → ≈ 7/128 ≈ 5.5%. p = 16 needs m ≫ 16 → ties global batch size to pipeline depth → why ZB/DualPipe exist.

Context + expert parallel

  • CP: whole sequence sharded; ring attention passes KV blocks round the ring. 2026: Megatron Bridge 0.6.0 trains GLM-5.2 at 128K context with THD-packed CP.
  • EP: all-to-all token routing in every MoE layer → why DeepSeek built DualPipe and why 72-GPU NVLink domains suit large-EP MoE.

Mapping onto the hardware

Rule (innermost → outermost): TP(+SP) → CP → EP → PP → DP/FSDP. The more often a group talks, the faster the link it needs.

  • TP: synchronous every layer → fastest link. 8-GPU nodes cap TP at 8; GB200/GB300 NVL72 = one 72-GPU NVLink domain (AWS P6e-GB200 UltraServers: 36 or 72).
  • PP ships only boundary activations → fine on 400 Gb/s. DP/FSDP overlaps with compute → outermost.
  • Llama 3 405B (reported): order [TP, CP, PP, DP], TP = 8 in-node, PP = 16, CP up to 16, DP up to 128.
  • Stacking pays (torchtitan, H100, vs optimized baselines): +65.08% 8B (128 GPUs, 1D), +12.59% 70B (256 GPUs, 2D), +30% 405B (512 GPUs, 3D).
Order mnemonic

"Tiny Cats Eat Pizza Daily" = TP, CP, EP, PP, DP, from the fastest link outwards. Chattiest group sits closest.

Collectives and the network

The collectives

  • reduce-scatter: sum, each rank keeps 1/N (FSDP grads). all-gather: everyone gets every shard (FSDP params).
  • all-reduce = reduce-scatter + all-gather → everyone has the full sum (DDP grads, TP activations).
  • all-to-all (MoE routing) and send/recv (PP boundaries).

Ring vs tree (NCCL)

ring all-reduce traffic per GPU = 2·(N − 1)/N × message ≈ 2 × messageindependent of N → bandwidth-optimal for big messages; latency grows with ring length
  • Tree: log-depth → better for small, latency-bound messages.
  • NCCL picks algorithm per message size + topology. "Bus bandwidth" (nccl-tests) = measured rate × that 2(N−1)/N factor → compare directly to link peak.
  • Defaults can be wrong: on GB200 NVL72 (3 NVL partitions, NVLS off) NCCL chose ring, but tree hit 485.68 vs 393.66 GB/s at 24 GPUs → benchmark your cluster.
  • NVSwitch can also reduce in-network (NVLS/SHARP).

Why bandwidth matters (worked)

  • 7B BF16 grads = 14 GB → ring all-reduce ≈ 28 GB sent per GPU per step.
  • At 50 GB/s (400 Gb/s) → ~0.56 s if not overlapped. NVLink 4 (~450 GB/s each way) → ~0.06 s.
  • 70B DDP-style → ~5.6 s per step across nodes ⇒ shard + overlap, HSDP, gradient accumulation (fewer syncs per token), TP/EP on NVLink.

Scale-up vs scale-out

What lower precision does not fix

  • FP8/FP4 speed up GEMMs (~2× / ~4× the BF16 peak on Blackwell) and shrink activations + comms.

How often to checkpoint: Young/Daly

W_opt ≈ √(2·μ·C), waste ≈ √(2C/μ) + R/μμ = job MTBF, C = blocking cost of one checkpoint, R = restart + reload time. First-order: assumes exponential failures and coordinated checkpoints (exact optimum uses Lambert W)
  • Textbook: μ = 4 h, C = 6 min → W ≈ 54 min.
  • Llama-3 scale, μ = 186 min:
    • C = 5 min (sync save) → W ≈ 43 min, ~23% waste
    • C = 1 min → W ≈ 19 min, ~10% waste
    • C = 6 s (async blocking part) → W ≈ 6 min, ~3% waste
    • R = 10 min adds ≈ 5% more
  • Levers: cut C (async), cut R + lost work (in-memory/peer replicas, checkpointless, hot spares, auto node replacement), raise μ (burn-in, health checks).
Young/Daly in words

"Checkpoint every root-two-mu-C." Halve the save cost → interval shrinks by √2 and waste drops by √2. Waste ∝ √C, so async saves pay off fast.

Checkpoint size + async saves

  • Full state ≈ 16 B/param → 1.1 TB (70B), 6.5 TB (405B) → ~65 s at 100 GB/s aggregate storage.
  • PyTorch DCP (torch.distributed.checkpoint): sharded, every rank writes its own piece in parallel. async_save: short GPU→CPU staging, write in background.
  • Results: >10× less checkpoint time (PyTorch blog); 5–15× less overhead vs sync (torchtitan paper, Llama 3.1 8B); a prototype overlapping the GPU→CPU copy with fwd/bwd → <1 s.
  • Newer DCP: process-based async, save-plan caching, rank-local checkpoints.

Reduce-scatter + all-gather. Ring sends 2(N − 1)/N × the message ≈ 2× the message, whatever N is.

Inference & Serving

Oct 2026

  • Prefill = whole prompt at once, compute-bound → sets TTFT. Decode = 1 token/step, memory-bandwidth-bound → sets ITL. Batching is the main lever; judge a deployment by goodput, not raw tok/s.
  • KV/token = 2 × layers × kv_heads × head_dim × bytes → it caps concurrency. Shrink it (GQA, MLA, FP8 KV), page it (PagedAttention), reuse it (prefix cache), offload it (LMCache, Dynamo KVBM).
  • Continuous batching + chunked prefill = standard. 2026 frontier = disaggregated prefill/decode (Dynamo 1.x, llm-d, SGLang, KServe).
  • Engines: vLLM (default), SGLang (shared prefixes, giant MoE), TensorRT-LLM (NVIDIA peak, now PyTorch-only). TGI = maintenance mode.
  • Defaults: FP8 weights + FP8 KV, NVFP4 on Blackwell, FlashAttention-4, EAGLE-3 speculative decoding (~2–3×, low batch only).
  • Managed APIs: on-demand, batch (~50% off), provisioned/priority, prompt caching (reads ~10% of input). Self-hosting wins only at high, steady utilization (→ Topic 9).

Metrics: what "fast" actually means

One request, two clocks: wait-to-start and talking speed. Measure them separately.

  • TTFT (time to first token) = queue + prefill (+ network). Grows with prompt length and scheduler contention.
  • TPOT (time per output token) = mean gap between tokens after the first. ITL (inter-token latency) = the distribution of those gaps. Often used interchangeably, but p99 ITL shows stalls that the TPOT mean hides.
  • Throughput = output tok/s per GPU or node, or req/s. Always pair it with p50/p90/p99 TTFT and ITL: throughput can always be bought with worse tails.
  • Goodput (DistServe, OSDI '24) = max req/s per GPU that meets both the TTFT and TPOT SLOs → the metric to size fleets with. DistServe: 7.4× more requests or 12.6× tighter SLOs than its baselines, >90% of requests in SLO.
E2E latency ≈ TTFT + (n_out − 1) × TPOTn_out = output tokens. e.g. a 500-token answer at 20 ms TPOT → ~10 s of decode after the first token

Reading benchmarks

  • Artificial Analysis (secondhand): output speed = median over a rolling 72 h window, default 10K-token input; TTFT reported separately. e.g. Llama 3.3 70B on Groq ≈ 297 tok/s, ≈ 1.01 s TTFT.
  • gpt-oss-120B (high reasoning) measured ~92 to ~1,916 tok/s across hosts → the host matters as much as the model.
  • MLPerf Inference v6.0 (Apr 1, 2026): Server = latency-bound, Poisson arrivals; Offline = pure throughput. Vendor-reported: GB300 NVL72 on DeepSeek-R1 = 575,580 / 673,936 tok/s (Server / Offline); 87 × MI355X on Llama 2 70B = 1,042,110 tok/s Offline (AMD's first >1M).
Remember: 2 clocks

"Start fast = prefill. Talk fast = decode." TTFT is a compute problem, ITL is a memory-bandwidth problem. Each needs a different fix.

Prefill vs decode & the roofline

Prefill: the whole prompt runs as one big batched matmul → many FLOPs per byte of weights → compute-bound. It builds the KV cache and emits the first token.

Decode: one token per sequence per step, yet every step re-reads all weights + the KV cache from HBM. At batch 1 the GEMMs have intensity ≈ 1 → ~1% of peak FLOPS → bandwidth-bound. More FLOPS do not speed up decode.

intensity = FLOPs ÷ bytes moved  ·  ridge = peak FLOPS ÷ HBM BWH100: 989 TFLOPS (dense BF16) ÷ 3.35 TB/s ≈ 295 FLOPs/byte. Below the ridge → bandwidth-limited; above → compute-limited

Why batching is (almost) free

  • Linear-layer intensity ≈ B (batch size in tokens): the same weight bytes serve B tokens. Batch 32 is still under the ridge, but gets ~30× more tokens per weight read than batch 1.
  • Crossover on H100 BF16: ~280 tokens of batch (one source); production batches of 16–32 usually stay memory-bound.
  • The catch: KV reads aren't shared. Each sequence reads its own cache → attention intensity stays ~1–4 even when batched. Long context ⇒ still bandwidth-bound.
TPOT ≥ (weight bytes + KV bytes read per step) ÷ HBM bandwidthlower bound for one memory-bound decode step

Worked (derived): 70B in FP8 ≈ 70 GB of weights ÷ 3.35 TB/s ≈ 21 ms/step → ≤ ~48 tok/s per stream at batch 1. At batch 64 the same read serves 64 tokens → ~3,000 tok/s aggregate (minus KV overhead) while per-user TPOT barely moves.

  • FP8 halves bytes (intensity ≈ 2B), but FP8 peak is also ~2× BF16 → crossover stays ~300. The win is less read time per step.
  • A single 2K-token prefill has intensity ~2,000 → it can fill the GPU alone → root cause of prefill/decode interference.
  • Hardware fit: compute-dense chips for prefill, SRAM-heavy / high-bandwidth chips (Cerebras, Groq) for decode → e.g. AWS pairing Trainium 3 prefill with Cerebras CS-3 decode.

KV cache: the memory that decides concurrency

Every earlier token's K and V, at every layer, kept in HBM so they aren't recomputed. Grows with context length × live sequences → KV, not weights, caps users per GPU.

KV/token = 2 × n_layers × n_kv_heads × head_dim × bytes2 = K and V. Total KV = KV/token × Σ (prompt + generated tokens) over all live sequences

Shrink it

  • GQA: groups of query heads share one K/V head (70B: 64 Q → 8 KV heads = 8× smaller than MHA). MQA: a single K/V head. Both can cost quality, which motivated MLA.
  • MLA (DeepSeek-V2/V3): caches one compressed latent per layer per token (+ a small RoPE key) → (512 + 64) × 61 layers × 2 B ≈ 69 KiB/token. V2 paper: KV −93.3%, 5.76× max generation throughput. ← vs DeepSeek 67B, not vs "generic MHA"
  • Quantized KV: FP8 = production baseline (½). NVFP4 KV (Blackwell): up to 50% below FP8, <1% accuracy loss (NVIDIA); dequantized to FP8 for the attention math.

Capacity planning (derived)

2 × 80 GB GPUs (TP=2), 70B FP8 weights (~70 GB) → ~70–80 GB left for KV after activations + CUDA-graph buffers → at 320 KiB/token ≈ 230–250K tokens ≈ ~30 concurrent 8K-context sequences. FP8 KV doubles that → the first lever before buying GPUs.

Page it: PagedAttention

  • Pre-paging engines reserved a contiguous max-length buffer per request → only ~20–38% of KV memory held real tokens (secondhand reading of the paper).
  • vLLM (SOSP '23): fixed-size blocks (default 16 tokens) allocated on demand + a block table, like OS pages → waste < 1 block per sequence, copy-on-write for parallel sampling and beam search → 2–4× throughput at the same latency vs FasterTransformer and Orca.

Reuse it: prefix caching

  • Same leading tokens → same KV → skip that part of prefill → lower TTFT. vLLM V1: on by default (hash per KV block).
  • SGLang RadixAttention: prefixes stored in a radix tree, so any shared branch is reused. "Up to 5×" on high-reuse workloads (secondary). v0.5.x added a decode-side radix cache for PD setups.
  • Shared content first, byte-identical (system → tools → docs → question). Managed-API prompt caching follows the same rule.

Batching & scheduling: static → continuous → disaggregated

  1. Static: a fixed batch runs to completion; short requests wait for the longest.
  2. Dynamic (Triton-style): collect up to size N or a timeout, then run to completion. Fine for fixed-shape models (CNNs, encoders), poor for generation.
  3. Continuous / in-flight (Orca 2022, vLLM; "in-flight" is TRT-LLM's term): the batch is re-formed every iteration; finished sequences leave, new ones join. Flaw: one long prefill hogs an iteration and all decodes wait = a generation stall (seconds, per Sarathi-Serve).
  4. Chunked prefill (Sarathi-Serve, OSDI '24): a per-iteration token budget, filled with decodes first, then prefill chunks → bounded ITL, slightly higher TTFT for long prompts. Up to 2.6× capacity in SLO vs vLLM (Mistral-7B); up to 5.6× for Falcon-180B with PP. vLLM's knob: --max-num-batched-tokens.
  5. Disaggregated P/D: separate GPU pools per phase. Prefill = compute (often higher TP); decode = bandwidth + KV capacity (wide EP for MoE). KV moves over RDMA/NVLink (NIXL, LMCache, Mooncake-style engines).

Speculative decoding

Decode leaves compute idle → a cheap draft guesses γ tokens; the target verifies all of them in one pass and keeps the accepted run + 1. The output distribution stays the target's → quality unchanged.

E[tokens per target pass] = (1 − α^(γ+1)) ÷ (1 − α)α = acceptance rate, γ = draft length (Leviathan et al., 2023). Speedup ≈ E ÷ (1 + γ·c), c = draft cost per target step
  • α 0.7, γ 5 → 2.94 tokens/pass; α 0.8 → 3.69.
  • EAGLE-3: a draft head that fuses hidden states from 3 intermediate target layers (EAGLE-1 used the last layer only) → c stays tiny.
  • Reality: ~2× on Llama-3.1-8B (α 0.6–0.8), 2–3× typical; one 8B test got 1.84× on code but 0.99× on chat. α below ~0.55 → net loss.
  • Fades at large batch: decode is already near the ridge, so verifying γ extra tokens costs real FLOPs → used mainly for low-latency tiers.
  • Draft types: small draft model · Medusa heads (tree attention) · EAGLE 1/2/3 · n-gram / prompt lookup (RAG, code edits) · MTP heads (DeepSeek-V3).

CUDA graphs & parallelism

  • CUDA graphs: record the decode step's kernels once per batch-size bucket, then replay → no CPU launch overhead (matters at small batch). vLLM V1 adds torch.compile fusions (e.g. RMSNorm + quant) and piecewise graphs with attention left outside.
  • TP: shard each layer; 2 all-reduces per layer → needs NVLink → TP ≤ 8 in a node (or one NVL72). Fewer weight bytes per GPU → lower TPOT + more KV room. PP: spans nodes, costs bubbles. EP: standard for big MoE, with data-parallel attention.

Before buying GPUs: FP8 weights → FP8 KV → cache-friendly prompt layout → tune --max-num-batched-tokens / --max-num-seqs against your SLOs → spec decoding only for low-batch traffic → then scale out with prefix-aware routing.

Deployment patterns: scale, load, route, fail over

Autoscaling signals

GPU utilization is a bad signal: decode keeps kernels busy, so it reads ~100% even at light load. Scale on:

  1. Queue depth: waiting requests per replica.
  2. KV-cache usage: sustained >90% → preemptions coming.
  3. TTFT / ITL p95 vs SLO.

Cold starts take minutes → weight streaming, pre-pulled images, a warm minimum of replicas. With P/D: scale prefill on TTFT, decode on ITL/KV. Scale-in needs graceful draining (KServe v0.19).

Weight loading

  • Run:ai Model Streamer: concurrent C++ reads from SSD / NFS / S3 straight into GPU memory; Safetensors as-is; beat the HF loader and Tensorizer in NVIDIA's tests. vLLM --load-format runai_streamer; Azure Blob via az://.
  • Storage layout matters too: 554 GiB TP16 model on Lustre, streamer + file striping → ~76 min → ~5 min.
  • Dynamo ModelExpress: weight streaming over NIXL/NVLink, up to 7× faster startup for big MoE (vendor).

Multi-LoRA serving

  • One base in GPU memory, many small adapters. S-LoRA: adapters in host RAM, Unified Paging of adapters + KV, mixed-rank kernels → thousands of adapters, up to 4× vs naive vLLM LoRA. Punica: SGMV batched kernel.
  • Engines now batch different adapters together; model-name routing maps the API model field → base + adapter (KServe v0.19, GAIE).

Routing: Gateway API Inference Extension

Self-check

Why doesn't 2× the FLOPS halve TPOT at batch 1?
Decode at batch 1 has intensity ≈ 1, far below the ~295 ridge, so HBM bandwidth (weights + KV per step) is the limit. Only more bandwidth, fewer bytes or bigger batches help.
KV per token for an 80-layer, 8-KV-head, head_dim-128 model in BF16? For a 128K sequence?
2 × 80 × 8 × 128 × 2 B ≈ 320 KiB/token → ≈ 40 GiB per 128K sequence (≈ 20 GiB with FP8 KV).
What does chunked prefill fix, and what does it cost?
Generation stalls from long prefills. A per-step token budget bounds ITL; long prompts pay slightly higher TTFT.
When is disaggregated prefill/decode not worth it?
Small deployments, where KV-transfer overhead and a fixed P:D ratio cost more than the interference removed.
EAGLE-3 with α = 0.7, γ = 5: tokens per target pass? Why can real speedup be ~1×?
≈ 2.94. Draft cost eats some of it, verification isn't free at high batch, and on chat α drops → break-even.
Three autoscaling signals better than GPU utilization?
Queue depth, KV-cache usage (>90% → preemptions), TTFT / ITL p95 vs SLO.
Why does the GAIE Endpoint Picker beat round-robin?
It routes on prefix affinity + live load, so requests land where their prefix KV already lives → skipped prefill, lower TTFT.
Claude API: when does a 5-minute cache write pay off? A 1-hour one?
From the 2nd request (1.35× vs 2×); 1-hour from the 3rd (2.2× vs 3×).

  • Rare releases, no CI/CD. "Deploy" = hand over a model artifact, not a pipeline.

MLOps vs LLMOps — what actually changes

Classic MLOps
  • Versioned: code, data, features, model weights.
  • Quality: offline accuracy / AUC on a holdout.
  • Monitor: feature vectors + prediction distribution (drift).

  • Loop: continuous training.
  • Output: deterministic predict().

  • Output: same input → different outputs → run several trials.

Observability: from drift to traces

Classic ML monitoring

  • Data drift (covariate shift): P(x) moves — inputs look different. Detectable without labels.
  • Concept drift: P(y|x) moves — same inputs, different right answer (e.g. new fraud patterns). Confirm only with labels or proxies, often delayed.
  • Also watch prediction drift, data quality (nulls, schema), delayed-label performance.
  • Tests: PSI, KS (numeric), chi-square (categorical), Wasserstein, Jensen-Shannon. Evidently: DataDriftPreset(method="psi").
PSI = Σᵢ (aᵢ − eᵢ) · ln(aᵢ / eᵢ)over bins i; aᵢ = actual (current) share in bin, eᵢ = expected (reference) share. Add a tiny ε to empty bins or ln blows up.
DKS = maxₓ | Fref(x) − Fcur(x) |two-sample KS = largest gap between the two empirical CDFs (numeric features)
PSI rule of thumb

< 0.1 → fine.
0.1–0.25 → moderate, look.
> 0.25 → significant, act (retrain trigger).

Evaluation: golden sets, judges, RAG & agents

Vocabulary (Anthropic, Jan 2026): task = inputs + success criteria · trial = one attempt · grader = scores one aspect · transcript = full record · outcome = final environment state (does the booking exist in the DB, not "did the agent say it booked").

Offline vs online

Offline

Golden set, run in CI before deploy. Same inputs every time → catches regressions. Blind to new prod behaviour.

Online

Sample prod traces, score with the same judges (MLflow 3.9 online judges), emit gen_ai.evaluation.result. Failures → back into the golden set.

"Swiss cheese" layers — each has holes, stack them: CI evals · prod monitoring · A/B tests · user feedback · transcript review · periodic human studies.

Building the golden set

  • Start with 20–50 tasks from real failures; turn bug reports + support tickets into tasks.
  • Error analysis first: read ~100 traces (saturation point), open-code failures, cluster into failure modes, prioritise.
  • Two experts should agree on each verdict; every task gets a reference solution; balance positive + negative cases.
  • Near-0% pass rate usually = broken task or grader, not a dumb model.

Graders

  • Code-based (string match, unit tests, outcome checks): fast, cheap, reproducible, brittle. Exhaust these first.
  • Model-based (rubrics, pairwise, multi-judge): flexible, but non-deterministic, costlier, needs calibration.
  • Human: gold standard, slow + expensive.
  • Grade outcomes, not paths; partial credit for multi-part tasks.

LLM-as-judge done right

  • Binary pass/fail, not Likert scales or letter grades (Hamel Husain's method).
  • One failure mode per judge, explicit definitions, few-shot examples; give an "Unknown" option.
  • Validate on held-out human labels: report TPR / TNR (~50 pass + 50 fail labels; another of his skills says ≥ 20 / 20 — numbers vary).
TPR = TP ÷ (TP + FN)  ·  TNR = TN ÷ (TN + FP)"pass" = positive. Corrected true pass rate θ ≈ (pobs + TNR − 1) ÷ (TPR + TNR − 1)

Biases (Zheng et al., MT-Bench, 2023): position, verbosity, self-enhancement, limited reasoning. GPT-4 judge: > 80% agreement with humans (≈ human–human), yet only 65.0% swap-consistent.

  • Position → swap order, both orders must agree, else tie.
  • Verbosity → length-controlled rubrics / penalise padding.
  • Self-enhancement → judge from a different model family than the generator.
  • Plus few-shot + reference-guided grading. MLflow: make_judge, judge.align() (MemAlign).

Agents: trials, pass@k vs pass^k

pass@k = 1 − (1 − p)k  ·  pass^k = pkp = per-trial success, trials independent. p = 0.75, k = 3 → pass@3 ≈ 98.4%, pass^3 ≈ 42%
Memory trick

pass@k = "at least once" → rises with k (research, coding retries).
pass^k = "every time" → falls with k → use for customer-facing consistency.

  • Coding agents: deterministic tests (SWE-bench Verified, Terminal-Bench).
  • Conversational: state checks, turn limits, tone rubric, simulated user (τ-Bench, τ2-Bench).
  • Research: groundedness, coverage, source quality (BrowseComp).
  • Computer use: URL / page / backend state (WebArena, OSWorld).

RAG end to end: documents → cited answer

Why: weights don't know your private or fresh data → retrieve at query time, put it in the prompt (Lewis et al. 2020). 2026 RAG = a multi-stage pipeline; each stage catches the previous one's misses.

1 · Ingest, parse, chunk

  • Connectors (S3 / GCS / Blob / SharePoint / Drive) → re-index on change. Layout-aware parsing: a table flattened to text soup → wrong numbers (Bedrock Data Automation, FM-as-parser, LlamaParse).
  • Metadata on every chunk (source, date, tenant, ACL) → query-time filters.
  • Fixed / recursive (paragraph → sentence → word, with overlap) = framework default; OpenAI file search uses 800 tokens, 400 overlap. Semantic: cut where neighbour-sentence similarity drops. Hierarchical: match the small child, return the big parent.
  • Late chunking (Jina 2024): embed the whole doc with a long-context embedder, then mean-pool token vectors per chunk span → doc-wide context, no training.
  • Rule of thumb: ~300–800 tokens with overlap.

2 · Contextual retrieval (Anthropic, Sept 2024; still the reference benchmark)

Chunk says "The company's revenue grew by 3%" → which company, which quarter? Fix: Claude 3 Haiku reads doc + chunk → writes a 50–100 token context, prepended before embedding and BM25. Prompt caching keeps the doc loaded → ≈ $1.02 per M doc tokens, one-time.

3 · Embed & index

  • Bi-encoder: docs embedded offline, query online → fast. Cross-encoder: (query, doc) scored jointly → accurate, slow. ColBERT (per-token vectors, MaxSim) sits between.
  • Matryoshka vectors truncate with graceful loss → storage/latency vs recall (OpenAI text-embedding-3 dimensions).
  • Always add BM25 → exact terms (error codes, SKUs, names). Cache embeddings by text hash → skip unchanged chunks.

4 · Retrieve: hybrid + RRF

RRF(d) = Σi 1 / (k + ranki(d))sum over result lists i (dense, BM25, sub-queries); ranks start at 1; k = 60 (Cormack et al. 2009)
  • Ranks, not raw scores → no normalising cosine vs BM25. Doc #1 dense + #3 BM25 → 1/61 + 1/63 ≈ 0.0323; #2 in dense only → 0.0161 ⇒ found by both wins.
  • Apply metadata + ACL filters here, at query time.

5 · Rewrite the query

  • HyDE (2022): LLM writes a hypothetical answer → embed it → fetch real docs near it. Zero-shot; answer-shaped text sits closer to answer docs than a short question.
  • Multi-query / decomposition: sub-queries → retrieve each → RRF (Bedrock KB "query decomposition"). Costs +1 LLM call of latency.

6 · Rerank, then generate with citations

  • Top 150 → cross-encoder rerank → keep 20. Top-20 beat 10 and 5, but too much context distracts → tune k. Managed: Cohere Rerank 3.5, Amazon Rerank 1.0 (Bedrock), Azure semantic ranker, Google Ranking API.
  • Chunks get IDs; "answer only from sources, cite IDs, else say not found". Anthropic Citations API (Jan 2025): cited_text spans, auto sentence-splitting, cited text not billed as output. Or a separate pass (Anthropic's CitationAgent).

Advanced RAG, failure modes & long context vs RAG

  • GraphRAG (Microsoft, Apr 2024): LLM extracts an entity graph → Leiden communities → LLM summarises each → global "main themes?" questions answered map-reduce. Beat vector RAG on comprehensiveness/diversity at ~1M tokens. LazyGraphRAG (Nov 2024) defers summaries to query time → indexing ~0.1% of GraphRAG's cost. Managed: Bedrock KB on Neptune Analytics. Pinpoint facts → hybrid + rerank is enough.
  • Agentic RAG: LLM plans queries, picks sources, searches in parallel, iterates. Azure agentic retrieval (Foundry IQ): decompose → parallel sub-queries → semantic rerank → cited results. Roots: Self-RAG (reflection tokens decide when to retrieve), Corrective RAG (grader → web search if docs are poor). Better multi-hop, more calls.

Seven failure points (Barnett et al. 2024) → fixes

  1. Missing content → coverage checks; allow "I don't know".
  2. Missed top-ranked docs → hybrid + contextual chunks + rewriting.
  3. Not in context (dropped at consolidation) → rerank, tune k.
  4. Not extracted (present, ignored) → fewer, better-ordered chunks.
  5. Wrong format → structured outputs.
  6. Incorrect specificity, 7. incomplete answers → decomposition + an eval set.

Lost in the middle (2023): recall is best at the start/end of a long context, worst in the middle → rerank, keep k small.

Cached long prompt

KB < 200K tokens (~500 pages), stable, one tenant → include it all. Caching → >2× lower latency, up to 90% cheaper.

RAG

Rate limits, retries & circuit breakers

  • Limits = RPM + tokens (OpenAI TPM; Anthropic ITPM + OTPM token bucket) → 429 + retry-after. Retry 429 / 500 / 503 / 529; never blindly retry a 400.
  • Why jitter: lockstep backoff → synchronised retry waves (thundering herd); random waits minimise contention and total work (Brooker 2015).
sleep = random(0, min(cap, base · 2attempt))"full jitter"; honour Retry-After first if the server sends it
import random, time

RETRYABLE = {429, 500, 503, 529}

def call_with_backoff(send, base=0.5, cap=30.0, max_attempts=6):
    for attempt in range(max_attempts):
        resp = send()
        if resp.status not in RETRYABLE:
            return resp                            # success or non-retryable
        ra = resp.headers.get("retry-after")       # server hint wins; else full jitter
        delay = float(ra) if ra else random.uniform(0, min(cap, base * 2 ** attempt))
        time.sleep(delay)
    raise RuntimeError(f"gave up after {max_attempts} attempts")
  • Circuit breaker per backend: Closed (count failures) → Open (fail fast → fallback) → Half-Open (trial requests after cooldown).
  • Fallback: same model, other region first (Bedrock cross-region inference, Azure global deployments, Vertex global endpoint) → then another model.
  • Streaming: SSE events (message_start … content_block_delta … message_stop) → fast first token, no proxy idle timeouts.
  • Structured outputs: constrained decoding to JSON Schema. OpenAI strict: true → 100% schema adherence vs < 40% before; Anthropic strict tool use (Nov 2025); MCP outputSchema. Valid ≠ correct.

Why prepend context before BM25 indexing too, not just before embedding?
Lost facts (company, period) are exact keywords; BM25 can then match them. Embeddings alone −35%; + BM25 −49%; + rerank −67%.

~15× chat tokens (single agent ~4×), and most of the gain comes from that spend. Poor fit for shared-context work such as most coding.

MCP: agent calls a server's tools/resources/prompts. A2A: agent delegates a task to another agent found via its Agent Card.

Lockstep clients retry in waves; uniform random waits under the exponential cap spread them out. Honour retry-after first.

Behaviour (prompts tuned per model) and the prompt cache (per model). Fall back to the same model in another region first.

Lethal trifecta + Agents Rule of Two

Lethal trifecta (Simon Willison, 16 Jun 2025): an agent with all three can be tricked into stealing data.

private data + untrusted content + external comms ⇒ exfiltration"external comms" includes rendering links or images

Agents Rule of Two (Meta, 31 Oct 2025; inspired by the trifecta + Chrome's Rule of 2): until injection can be reliably detected, give an agent at most two of:

|{A, B, C}| ≤ 2 → may run autonomously; = 3 → human approvesA = untrusted input · B = sensitive systems / private data · C = changes state or communicates externally
  • Goes further than the trifecta: [C] covers state-changing actions (delete, pay, deploy), not just exfiltration.
  • Both are heuristics that shrink blast radius, not proofs. OWASP researchers still call injection unsolved.
Memory trick

"Pick two", like fast / cheap / good: read the internet, hold secrets, act in the world. Want the third → a human signs off.

Supply chain & denial of wallet

  • Pickle checkpoints (.bin, .pt) run code on load → "executable code you are calling a model".
  • nullifAI (ReversingLabs, Feb 2025): Hugging Face models with broken or 7z-compressed pickles. torch.load() wrapper failed → Picklescan errored without reading the payload, but the payload still ran. Scale disputed (removed within 24 h vs "closer to proof-of-concept").
  • Blacklist scanners over an executable format always leave a gap → safetensors: tensor data only, no code path by design. Research for pickles you can't avoid: PickleBall (arXiv 2508.15987).

Remember

Signed ≠ safe. Scanned ≠ safe. Safetensors removes the code path; signing tells you who; only evals + review tell you what.

Denial of wallet (LLM10)

A billing problem as much as a security one: an attacker or a runaway agent loop burns tokens, or harvests outputs to extract the model.

worst-case spend/hr ≈ req/s cap × 3600 × max tokens/req × $/tokene.g. 10 req/s × 4,000 max output tokens = 40k tok/s ≈ 144M tokens/hr, times your price

Controls: per-user + per-tenant rate limits, max-token caps on every call, quotas, budget alarms, max agent steps per task.

Defenses: layer them, architecture first

  1. Architecture: CaMeL-style plan/data split, Rule of Two, no auto-rendered external URLs.
  2. Least privilege: per-user on-behalf-of (OBO) tokens, scoped tools, read-only by default.
  3. Containment: sandboxes, egress allowlists, budgets.
  4. Detection: Prompt Shields / Bedrock prompt-attack filter / Llama Guard.
  5. Assurance: garak / PyRIT in CI, ATLAS-mapped threat model, logging.

Ordered by reliability. "You cannot filter your way out of prompt injection" → classifiers are layer 4, not layer 1.

GPU utilization: the real price is $/hr ÷ util

Measure the right thing

  • nvidia-smi "GPU-Util" = % of the sample window (≈ 1 s on many products) in which ≥ 1 kernel was running. A tiny kernel on one SM reads the same as one filling the chip → 100% with most SMs idle.
  • DCGM DCGM_FI_PROF_SM_ACTIVE = fraction of time ≥ 1 warp was active on an SM, averaged over all SMs. e.g. two GPUs both at 100% DCGM_FI_DEV_GPU_UTIL: A uses 2 of 16 SMs → SM activity ≈ 0.13; B uses all 16 → ≈ 1.
  • Dashboard: SM activity (or GR_ENGINE_ACTIVE) + SM occupancy + tensor-pipe activity. SM activity ≥ 0.8 is necessary, not sufficient.
  • Wrong both ways: high util → false confidence; low util → you blame the GPU when host, storage or network is the bottleneck. Short kernels fall between samples.

MIG
  • Hardware partitions: own memory, fault + QoS isolation

  • → production multi-tenant serving
Time-slicing
  • Pods take turns on the whole GPU
  • More workloads per GPU
  • No memory, fault or security isolation
  • → trusted dev/test
MPS
  • Kernels from several processes run at once

  • → trusted workloads

Lever 1: caching

  • 5-min write 1.25×, 1-h write 2×, read 0.1× (0.05× Opus/Sonnet 5.5, 0.025× Fable 5.1) → 5-min cache pays back after 1 read, 1-h after 2.

  • TTL: turns seconds apart → 5-min is 15–19% cheaper than 1-h; if 1 turn in 10 follows a pause → 5-min costs 54% more.
